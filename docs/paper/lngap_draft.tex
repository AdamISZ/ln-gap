\section{Abstract}

We propose, without any consensus change to Bitcoin, offchain bilateral contracts in Bitcoin that depend on arbitrary computation, calling this general paradigm LN-GAP (Lightning Network governed by arbitrary programs). That general idea is not new but we describe here a distinct mechanism to achieve O(1)-transactions to resolve disputed contracts onchain (that is, there is no onchain bisection), based on a \emph{venue}: a committee of validators that acts as a public clock for each contract, not as a ledger. The onchain dispute resolution depends on a threshold attestation of the validators to absence of a published entry, and a 1 of $n$ positive signature over the entry in a novel way (we call this an elliptic curve one time signature, or EC-OTS), and in so doing allows the application of the ForceMove paradigm for the offchain game with an independent timeout clock that does not rely on facts about the Bitcoin chain itself (thus not requiring Bitcoin SPV proofs). The advantages of this construction are: publishing entries requires only 1 of $n$ liveness, contracts are fully denominated in bitcoin and trust-minimised: they rely on the honesty of a majority of the venue's validators (a committee that can be drawn at random, per contract, from a large bonded roster), whose misbehaviour is attributable and who themselves are not required to know anything about the bilateral contract details, and fraudulent equivocation is provable. Those validators are motivated by both positive fees (paid, in bitcoin, atomically with publication in LN channels) and negatively by bonds (timelocked funds, whose owners lose their future fees when fraud is attributed, and which can optionally be made burnable).

\section{Introduction}

Start from the \textbf{known good} - that Lightning as a payments technology works, and works well. It aids privacy and scalability, if not perfectly addressing both, it addresses them to a large extent, at the cost of extra infrastructure for users and service providers. Once that infrastructure is in place, the \emph{payments} UI/workflow for users is as smooth as it should be\footnote{For those readers who balk at this characterization, note that the author has been paying his utility bills for years, and along with thousands of other payments during that time with near zero payment failures, on self-custodial Lightning, and for practical, not ideological, reasons, far prefers using LN as a payment technology over any existing fiat rail.}.

What's missing? Lightning already provides private, fast and cheap payments. In vague terms, I would argue that all it really misses is ``programmatic extensibility''. Take as an example this goal:

\begin{quote}
The customer wants to pay a storage service provider once per day, in sats, but wants the service provider to prove that they are upholding their agreement (with a response to a challenge for an inclusion proof), and if they fail to do so after a time window, their deposit can be taken.
\end{quote}

This is basically ``\underline{Filecoin without an altcoin}'', as designed in this paper (Section~\ref{sec:storage}).

\subsection{The problem of 2-party games on Lightning}

Suppose you want to play a game on Lightning, like chess, for a monetary reward, but don't want to have to trust anyone; the money should move specifically according to the agreed game rules.

I mention chess not because it's a good game to bet on (it isn't!) but because it's a game that's sufficiently complex that expressing it in onchain transactions (most specifically, proving a that move is illegal; proving that a move is legal would be way worse!) gets very expensive and we want to avoid it.

That such games can be played on chain at all, in today's Bitcoin, was shown by Poelstra~\cite{poelstrastate}: each player signs the next game state with a one-time (Lamport) signature, which Script can read, and a script checks that successive states are related by the rules, so tic-tac-toe needs 27 pre-signed transactions rather than one per path through the game tree (about 550,000), and the same approach extends in principle to chess. His distinction between ``Small Script'', expressive arithmetic on 32-bit values that cannot see the transaction, and ``Big Script'', which can, through \texttt{OP\_CHECKSIG}, is a useful lens for what follows: the predicates that judge a move here are Small Script over one-time-signed state, and only the payout is Big Script. What that work leaves open is the one-move-per-transaction pace of play on chain, which is the subject of the rest of this section.

One approach to doing this offchain is called ForceMove~\cite{forcemove}. The idea is relatively simple: behave optimistically, and fallback to onchain transactions as the arbiter if your opponent does not follow the rules you agreed to. The reason for its name is that, once on-chain, the opponent is \emph{forced to move} by having a timeout expressed in the blockchain's consensus logic. So in Bitcoin this would mean that the Script controlling the funds has a shape like: 

\[
\texttt{<}\,T_1\,\texttt{>}\ \ \texttt{OP\_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY}\ \ \texttt{OP\_DROP}\ \ \texttt{<}\,P_A\,\texttt{>}\ \ \texttt{OP\_CHECKSIG},
\]

where $P_A$ is the key of the counterparty not on-move, i.e. they win by default if their opponent doesn't move.

This seems to be the right general paradigm: if you're forced to move, the game cannot stall, but your 'move' can be of a type constructed to make it easy to prove fraud whenever the move is illegal. (I haven't bothered to note how terminal states are resolved, i.e. win/lose/draw; that's less important and pretty simple, usually).

\subsubsection{ForceMove is not the full solution}

ForceMove is clearly ``correct''; there is no way to have the game be fair without such a timeout clause. So if you lose the ability to publish within a time window, you forfeit the game. Note that this is exactly the same tradeoff that Lightning itself makes for the case of simple payments.

But the problem with ForceMove, which is not exposed in the Lightning payments case, is that the amount of interaction forced on-chain is potentially huge, depending on the game. Consider this scenario:

\begin{itemize}
\item Alice and Bob agree to play chess offchain, set up a contract in a Lightning channel (details deferred till later)
\item Alice makes move 1 as a message offchain, both sides cosign and state is updated; it's Bob's move.
\item Bob stalls, does not send his move
\item Alice is forced to resolve onchain: sends the state in a transaction, the output has a timeout forcing Bob to respond
\item Bob \emph{does} respond, with a valid move \ldots
\end{itemize}

\ldots now Alice is stuck playing the game onchain; a chess game can easily take 50-100 moves by both sides. If Bob is adversarial he will know that Alice will not want either the transaction cost nor especially the delay implicit in doing all of those moves onchain (she intended to play offchain, which can be near immediate), so he could bribe her. This would be much more relevant if the game is really financially motivated (\$ 1000 bet), and in case where we are at move 30 and Bob knows for sure he's losing; instead of resigning he says to Alice ``here, I'm generous, take \$ 800 and I'll take \$ 200, otherwise, we're going to chain''.

The point is not that that scenario is particularly realistic, the point is that \textbf{going onchain is a viable threat for an adversary}, in general, and that's why I say ``ForceMove is not the full solution''.

\subsubsection{Can we do ForceMove offchain?}

The next natural step. If we want to enforce a timeout clause like the above, but \emph{offchain}, then clearly putting it into a Bitcoin Script is possible, but that returns us precisely to where we started: ForceMove's forced move is only effected on-chain in the adversarial case; the thing we were trying to avoid.

There's a nuance here worth being precise about:

\begin{itemize}

\item If one side forces on-chain, publishing a dispute transaction, and says ``the other guy didn't follow the rules! here's proof'', with a signed attestation by the counterparty of an illegal move, no problem: the court can adjudicate the dispute.
\item If one side forces-on chain, publishing a dispute transaction, and says ``the other guy didn't send his move'', that's very different. How does the ``court'' (blockchain) know whether the move was sent or not? Clearly that's impossible, so the court has to treat both sides fairly, and insist they play out their moves. \uline{We have reverted to exactly the same problem we had in vanilla ForceMove}.
\end{itemize}

From this reasoning it's clear: to solve the problem of that last case, we need an \uline{objective fact} that the court can assess, not hearsay. That objective fact must take the form ``by the count of some external attestation, the counterparty did not post a move before the deadline''. In the next section we'll examine two different ways to create that external attestation.

The following diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:highlevel}) shows at a high-level how this logic can be enforced in a single ``contract output'' embedded in a Lightning channel. The most important point is that ``the counterparty did not move before the deadline'' is being attested to by an external ``venue''.

\subsection{From a proof of publication ledger to a venue}

The design began, as this section does, from the idea of a separate
proof of publication ledger, and the options below are ledgers. It
turned out that a ledger is more than a two-party contract needs. No
order across moves or across contracts is used, and no reader other
than the two players ever consults a contract's record, so no history
needs to be unique: what each move needs is that it was made available,
and a decision whether that happened by its due time. Uniqueness of a
move comes from the mover's own one-time key, not from the record. We
therefore call the service a \emph{venue}, and the combination of
publication with deadlines a \emph{public clock}
(Section~\ref{sec:beyond}); a committee can be drawn per contract
(Section~\ref{sec:roster}), and uniqueness in the sense of a
single-use seal is needed only for facts that many contracts share
(Section~\ref{sec:shared}).

Bitcoin itself gives proof of publication. That is of course a well known primitive by now, and exemplified in the simple, but useful, OpenTimestamps project~\cite{ots}. As discussed earlier, it's not practical to use this to adjudicate onchain, because Bitcoin SPV proofs in Script are not practical. If we make a separate one, we could use proof of work as a consensus mechanism for deciding a canonical chain, or we could use a set of bonded validators, where the security assumption is: if members of the quorum equivocate on the state, their bond can be slashed. This is the equivalent economic security assumption of the reorg threat in the proof of work variant. Let's consider PoW and bonded validator\footnote{We are not calling it proof of stake, because while the consensus algorithm may be similar, it's a notably different security construction than one in which the stake is the tokens of the ledger itself; here the ``stake'' is the bond \emph{in Bitcoin}, a timelocked slashable-to-burn output on the Bitcoin blockchain itself.}.

\subsubsection{Proof of Work}

As already noted, proving inclusion in Bitcoin's PoW chain with its SHA2 linked chain of headers is not practical in Bitcoin Script, without new opcodes (for example CAT for Merkle trees; we defer the details to many other papers and discussions). But since we are designing from scratch, we are not limited to that.

Considering that arithmetic in Bitcoin Script is more or less limited to what Poelstra calls ``Small Script''~\cite{poelstrastate}, which operates on 4 byte numbers, what we can do is design the proof of work algorithm to use nibble arithmetic only. This is realized in our sample code, in the \texttt{n4bit} crate of the proof of concept~\cite{lngapcode}. Note that there had already been a very similar construction made in 2025 by AbdelStark, see StackSat-128~\cite{stacksat}. So in general terms, imagine the proof of work hashing uses this nibble based algo, such that proving a hash chain is correct in Bitcoin Script is actually feasible. This would mean a contract participant can post something to the proof of publication ledger, and if their counterparty challenges, saying ``you didn't publish anything'', they can prove that in fact they did, in Script, thus winning the dispute over the pot.

Consider reorgs and canonical history: the key feature of proof of work is that the canonical history is defined by the heaviest chain. So imagine: Alice says ``Bob didn't publish his move'', then Bob says ``yes I did: it's in this PoP ledger block at this height, and here's the header chain back to genesis/checkpoint''. Alice can counter-refute: ``no you didn't: here's an alternate header chain with more weight, proving that yours is not canonical''. This is of course both necessary but also shows the fragility: necessary because otherwise Bob can "mine" a super-cheap chain that costs almost no PoW, fragile because without the kind of enormous weight of something like Bitcoin's PoW chain, reorgs could be easy and frequent even without adversarial behavior.

The least desirable aspect of the PoW option is in there: it's that this costs significant resources to maintain, otherwise it offers no security at all. How are miners compensated for this work? Merge mining seems not to apply straightforwardly, but the biggest count against this idea is the following:

\textbf{There is not a simple transaction fee paying process}, since all contracts are settled in Lightning channels (one can concoct something here, but it's not simple); the contract participants can, by the very nature of proof of work's lottery, never know in advance who the miner will be. If the ledger is not a blockchain with its own coin and own set of consensus rules, there is no viable way to create a positive incentive to include publications.

\subsubsection{Bonded validator quorum}

The alternative is to have a quorum of bonded validators decide what was published.

It turns out that with careful thought, it's possible to construct a version of this that is both sufficiently efficient to enforce the necessary rules in Script, and also that has the right kind of both positive and negative incentives, purely measured in bitcoin, without inserting a new altcoin.

A naive idea of how to do this hits a snag: Bitcoin Script can read Winternitz signatures in a couple of kilobytes; weighty but OK. It cannot, directly, read Schnorr or BLS signatures on arbitrary messages (the former without CHECKSIGFROMSTACK, the latter .. well that would be a much bigger consensus update!). An aggregate signature (FROST, BLS) would let a whole quorum be checked at once, but it cannot say which members signed; and to assess 66 out of 100 Winternitz signatures in a Bitcoin script seems patently absurd.

Both problems go away once we look at what the validators actually have to say. The quorum never has to sign the \emph{content} of a move. One member attests the entry (the positive statement, 1 of $n$), and the quorum says only one bit per move: ``not published by its due time''. A one-bit statement costs one key per member, a point whose secret the member reveals when the statement holds, and $t$ of $n$ such reveals are counted in Script with \texttt{OP\_CHECKSIGADD}, each naming the member who made it. The threshold is a count of named one-bit signatures, not a threshold signature, so it is attributable by construction. (A hash preimage per member would serve equally well for the count.)

The content attestation, by the one member, must also be read by Script, and here too a hash-based one-time signature would suffice: a table of preimages per (chunk, value), revealing those for the entry's values. We use instead an elliptic-curve version of the same one-time idea, which we call EC-OTS (Section~\ref{sec:graph:math}): the venue publishes an \emph{anticipation point} per (chunk, value) and seals an entry by revealing the discrete logs of the points it selects, and Script checks possession of each revealed scalar with one \texttt{OP\_CHECKSIG}. The reason for the curve is not Script but payment. Scalars and points add, so the attestation of a whole entry by a particular member collapses into a single point: the sum of the points the entry selects plus that member's proposer point, whose discrete log becomes known only when that member seals exactly that entry. That is an ordinary adaptor point, so the mover can pay the member for sealing, atomically, with a pre-signed transaction that the member's scalar completes (Section~\ref{sec:graph:fee}). This is the shape of a DLC, whose anticipation points exist so that transactions can be made conditional on an oracle's outcome; here the outcome is ``this entry was sealed, by this member'', and the transaction is the member's fee. Hash preimages do not add, and a lock on a whole entry's worth of them would be a special output checking every one in Script, not a point a payment can be locked to.

What you need, then:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Liveness} : can never be promised, just like in a proof of work chain, but if \emph{any} member of the validator set can enter your publication in a slot, then some approximate ``1 of $n$ liveness'' can be claimed, and this is an acceptable security model.
\item \textbf{A large enough quorum}: Credibility of the above as well as what follows, requires some relatively large, heterogeneous and ideally permissionless-entry set of validators. At one key per member per move, a quorum of 50 of 100 is practical in Script. Even figures 10 times higher than that are \emph{theoretically} possible, but in practice we would not go much higher.\footnote{Section~\ref{sec:accountable} returns to this: a large, dynamic set need not be verified in Script at all, if its role is to make a small, Script-legible committee accountable.}
\item \textbf{Fraud is attributable}: this is why the quorum's statements are one-bit signatures by named members and not a threshold signature. A FROST-style signature by its nature cannot reveal which members of the validator set committed the fraud, and punishing the entire set when only a threshold of it committed the fraud is clearly unworkable.
\item \textbf{Positive incentives in bitcoin}: this is possible in a more general ``sidechain'' model (pay fees to move around coins on the sidechain), but this is not a sidechain, nor any kind of blockchain: the only thing the validators decide is whether each publication was made in time; the semantics of the publications are none of their business. Here we have validators post bonds on bitcoin (timelocked coins) and receive fees for their publication efforts, atomically with those publications, over Lightning channels, using the adaptor points above. This incentivizes honest participation and disincentivizes fraud or dishonesty.
\end{itemize}

You may be wondering why we insist on \emph{attributable} fraud. Without attribution, users of the venue cannot discern which members they do not want to use in future, if something fraudulent is published. With attribution, a member caught contradicting itself is ejected: it loses its future fees while its bonded coins (coins locked to a CLTV output) sit out their timelock. Timelocked coins as a costly identity are not new: JoinMarket's fidelity bonds~\cite{fidelity} use exactly such outputs to make a Sybil attack on its market of coinjoin makers expensive, and are, to our knowledge, the only deployed use of timelocked bitcoin for that purpose; Section~\ref{sec:roster} returns to them for a roster that anyone can join. It's possible to go further, as an option: equivocation evidence can be made to unlock the bonded coins to anyone, but without covenants this is not a simple burn, it is a miner fee-race burn: since the evidence is public, any miner can include its own spend paying the whole bond as fee, so the money game-theoretically ends up with the miner of the first eligible block, and the cheater recovers it only by mining that block itself. We do not make this the baseline (Section~\ref{sec:venue}).

\subsection{What the venue attests to}

We'll continue to use the two simple motivating examples: tic-tac-toe, and chess. We'll discuss external facts, i.e. something not limited to a 2 player game, in Section~\ref{sec:beyond}.

What would an attestation look like? Probably a variant of:

\[ (S, m, S', t) \]

where we set a notation of $S$ for the game's internal state (e.g. chess: piece positions on the 64 square board plus a couple other details like castling), $m$ for the move being presented, and $t$ an optional extra defining ``terminal'' state: undefined, win A, win B, draw etc. depending, again, on the game.

Specific cases \emph{may} justify condensing this; the most natural one might be removing $S$, the state \emph{before} the player's move.

Each player attests to a move choice, of course; but there's a subtlety that affects the question of how condensed the game state $S$ attested to should be. In the case of tictactoe it can be as few as 20 or so bits; in the case of chess it can be \~ 35 bytes.



The subtlety is that the court sees nothing but what the dispute puts in
front of it. When the claimant disputes a move, the only facts available
in Script are the venue's attestations of two consecutive entries: the
one at the disputed depth and the one before it. So the attested state
must be \emph{self-sufficient}: everything the rules need in order to
judge a move must be recoverable from the pair $(S, S')$ plus the move
itself, with no appeal to the rest of the game's history. For
tic-tac-toe this is trivial (the board and whose turn it is). For chess
it means the state carries castling rights, the en-passant square, the
side to move and a status field, and it rules out adjudicating rules
that need the whole history (threefold repetition) unless a digest of
that history is itself part of the state.

Once the state is self-sufficient, the natural condensation follows: an
entry need carry only $(m, S', t)$, since the previous depth's entry
already carries $S$. The court reads both entries out and judges the
triple $(S, m, S')$. This is the form we use: each entry's \emph{head}
(48 bytes) holds the new state, the move and the status, the region the
mover signs; the entry carries the mover's one-time signature over that
region beside the head; and a dispute reads out the heads at depths $d-1$
and $d$. The mover's signature matters as much as the venue's: a venue
attestation proves an entry was \emph{published}, the mover's one-time
signature proves the mover \emph{made} it, and a refutation needs both,
or anyone could answer a claim with any junk the venue happened to
seal.

What the court needs from the venue is therefore exactly three kinds of
statement per move of each contract, and nothing about the game:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{this} entry is move $d$ of contract $c$ (a positive
  attestation, by whichever member sealed it);
\item the member who sealed it was member $i$ (so that it can be
  paid, and blamed);
\item move $d$ was not published by its due time (a negative statement,
  by a majority of members).
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:venue} describes how the venue produces these, and
Section~\ref{sec:graph} how a Bitcoin script consumes them.

\section{The venue}
\label{sec:venue}

The venue is a set of $n$ bonded members that attest the moves of the
contracts registered with it. It is not a blockchain: it has no coin,
executes nothing, keeps no global order and no sequence of slots. It
answers, per move of each contract, the two questions a dispute asks:
\emph{what} was published, and \emph{whether it was published in time}.
The two answers come from different mechanisms. In the terms of Discreet
Log Contracts~\cite{dlc}, the venue is a federation of oracles: each
move's content is an outcome attested by one member under anticipation
points published in advance, and each move's timeliness is a one-bit
outcome attested by a majority. What is new is not the attestation but
what is attested (publication and, above all, absence), and that the
attestations are read out in Script so that the game's rules can judge
the content.

\paragraph{Not a federation.} Both this venue and a federated sidechain
such as Liquid~\cite{liquid} rest on a majority, but not on the same one. A federation
holds the coins: its keys sign every withdrawal, so its majority can
move any funds, and can freeze them. The venue holds no coins and
decides one bit per move, whether it was published in time; it cannot
forge a move, which the player signs, nor change a rule, which Script
enforces, nor stop anyone leaving, since every user's funds sit in its
own channel and a unilateral close is always available. A federation is
trusted with the money; the venue only with the clock.
Section~\ref{sec:security} develops the comparison.

The proof of concept has one fixed set of members;
Section~\ref{sec:roster} proposes drawing each contract's members from
a larger bonded roster instead.

\subsection{What: attestations keyed by contract and move}

A contract $c$ registers with the venue for moves $d = 1..M$, and the
venue publishes, for every $(c, d)$, a \emph{content table}: under the
venue's \emph{content key}, a Schnorr key $X$ that every member holds,
one anticipation point $A_{(c,d),j,v}$ per header chunk $j$ and nibble
value $v$. Sealing move $d$ of contract $c$ is attesting its entry under
that table: revealing the discrete logs of the points the entry's
header selects. The attestation is an EC-OTS; Section~\ref{sec:graph:math}
gives the construction. Its property for our purposes is that a Bitcoin
script can check, one \op{CHECKSIG} per chunk, that the venue attested a
particular value at a particular position, with no signature
verification over arbitrary messages. Because the table is keyed by the
move and not by any schedule of the venue's, many contracts share one
venue, each with its own timing.

Because the content key is shared, \emph{any} member can seal any move.
Each move has a \emph{designated} sealer by rotation,
$p(c,d) = (h(c) + d) \bmod n$ with $h$ a hash of the contract's
identifier; the mover submits its signed entry to that member and, if no
seal appears within a backoff $b$ (seconds, covering propagation), to the
next member in the rotation, and so on. Inclusion therefore needs only
one honest, live member within the move's time (``1 of $n$ liveness'').
A shared key cannot say who used it, so each member also holds, per
move, a \emph{proposer} key $P_{i,(c,d)} = p_{i,(c,d)}G$ and reveals
$p_{i,(c,d)}$ in the seal it makes. Every refutation on Bitcoin names the
member whose seal it relies on (Section~\ref{sec:graph:claim}).

A seal counts only if it reaches the members before the move's due time,
since they flag what they have not seen. Seals are public and
self-authenticating, so the mover publishes every seal it receives to
all members (and to any audit layer, Section~\ref{sec:accountable}): a
sealer that reveals its seal only to the mover is defeated by the
mover's own dissemination. Near the deadline the mover may also submit
to several members in parallel, which is safe because seals of one entry
are byte-identical and differ only in their proposer reveal: a sealer
that delays its seal until the last moment is defeated by the others.
Withholding or delaying therefore works only if every member does it,
which is the 1-of-$n$ assumption again.

Honest members seal only entries the mover signed: the contract's
registration carries the movers' per-move state keys, and a member
checks the signature (never the move's legality, which is the disprove
family's business). A member that seals an unsigned entry is caught by
the entry itself, and ejected; the seal is inert in any contract, since
a refutation must carry the mover's signature.

Two seals of the same move are therefore one of four things: the same
entry sealed by two members (byte-identical attestations: the shared
key and the per-value nonces are deterministic); two different entries
both signed by the mover (the \emph{mover's} equivocation, answered by
the contract's \texttt{equiv} leaf); an unsigned entry (the sealer's
fault, provable); or a seal made after the due time (next). None needs
a canonical choice between them, so the venue needs no fork-choice rule.

\subsection{When: due times and flags}

Each contract fixes its timetable at open: move $d$ is due at
$T_d = t_0 + d\ell$ (seconds per move $\ell$; other rules, up to a chess
clock, are possible, since only the members read them). Members judge
``due'' against their own clocks, which need agree only to within
ordinary clock skew. The negative statement is a one-bit signature: each
member holds, per move, a \emph{flag} key $F_{i,(c,d)} = f_{i,(c,d)}G$
and reveals $f_{i,(c,d)}$ if at $T_d$ it held no attested, mover-signed
entry for the move. A flag is a single point whose secret is revealed or
not, so a script counts flags with \op{CHECKSIGADD} at the cost of one key
per member. A refutation relying on a seal that $t$ members flagged is
killed by the \texttt{not\_timely} leaf. The threshold is a bare
majority, $t = \lceil (n+1)/2 \rceil$: a flag is one-sided (false
absence costs $t$ named flaggers; a late seal left standing costs its
sealer plus the silence of $n - t + 1$ honest members), and the two
failure costs balance at the majority.

This is what makes timeliness enforceable against a colluding member.
Honest members do not seal a move after its due time; a member that
does, for a stalling player, produces a perfectly valid attestation,
and without flags a refutation built on it would defeat an honest
absence claim. With flags, the honest majority's on-time statement
``move $d$ was not published'' outweighs the late seal.

Bitcoin's clock is read in one place: the earliest time an absence claim
for move $d$ may be broadcast, an absolute timelock at $T_d + m$ against
median-time-past, the margin $m$ covering the lag of median-time-past
and a miner's freedom to skew timestamps. All other windows in a dispute
are relative timelocks, where block granularity only slows a resolution.
The per-move clock runs at the venue's pace; Bitcoin's clock measures
only the $O(1)$ dispute about it.

\subsection{A better-conditioned oracle problem}
\label{sec:oracle}

The venue is a federation of DLC oracles, but it attests a different
kind of fact from the one DLCs are usually imagined for, a price at
maturity, and the difference matters for security. The key property is
that the fact concerns the \emph{action of the party who benefits from
it}. A mover's entry, posted in an \texttt{OP\_RETURN}, proves that the
mover published it by that block: the mover signed it and Bitcoin dates
it. A price posted to Bitcoin proves only that someone claimed that
price. Publication is \emph{self-certifying}; a price is not, since
nobody can produce evidence of ``the price at noon'', only report it.

Three things follow in the venue's favour. The honest party can
strengthen the truth: a mover disseminates its seal to every member,
to an audit layer or to Bitcoin (Section~\ref{sec:accountable}), so a
lie must override evidence that is widely held, while a party wronged
by a price oracle can do nothing to make the true price more evident.
The reference cannot be manipulated: a price DLC can be attacked
without touching its oracle, by moving a thin market at the fixing
moment, while the venue's ``underlying'' is the mover's own action, and
the only analogue is an attack on the network. And the oracle decides
less: a price attestation \emph{is} the payout, while the venue attests
only publication and timing, and the game's rules judge the content
cryptographically.

The price for this is paid elsewhere. A price oracle attests one event
for every contract that uses it, so a lie in one party's favour is told
to the whole world, visibly; the venue attests per contract, so a
colluding majority can act against one contract silently. Submission
is private between mover and member, so a false flag is attributable
but, without a public record, not provably false, where a blatant price
lie is evident to all. And a price DLC needs its
oracle once, at maturity, while a game needs the venue at every move.
The self-certifying property also covers only the positive fact: for
absence, nobody can produce evidence unless every entry goes to a
public record, and the venue's trust concentrates there.

At slow timescales the two converge: a daily closing price across many
exchanges and a move visible to an audit layer within the hour are both
effectively objective, and at fast timescales both blur, through
manipulation or through propagation. Objectivity alone therefore does
not separate them; who can produce the evidence does. The shared facts
of Section~\ref{sec:shared} are of the price kind, and inherit its
profile: broadcast and untargetable, but with no party able to prove
them.

\subsection{The registry}

Everything a contract needs from the venue is fixed at open. The
contract's \emph{registry} lists the members, their proposer and flag
points for every move, and the content tables, each co-signed by every
member; the contract pins it by root. Members see a contract's
identifier, timetable and movers' state keys, never its content: they
publish points in advance and scalars when they seal or flag, and
whoever disputes uses them. Contract identifiers must be unique on a
venue (two contracts sharing one would share tables), and registration
is paid, since it costs the venue a table's curve arithmetic per move:
about 100\,KB of points and 100\,ms of computation per move, so some
10\,MB and 10\,s for a contract of a hundred moves. Tables cannot be
shared between contracts (the one-time property forbids it); attesting
only the 48-byte head rather than the whole header would halve the
cost.

\subsection{Incentives}

\paragraph{Fees.} A mover pays the member that seals its move,
atomically, with a point-locked payment inside the mover's channel. The
lock point is $T = C_h + P_{i,s}$, the sum of the content points the
mover's head selects and the intended sealer's proposer point; its
discrete log is revealed exactly when member $i$ seals exactly that
head as that move (Section~\ref{sec:graph:fee}). Publication and payment
are one event, as a preimage and a payment are one event in Lightning.

The designated sealer is generally not the payer's channel counterparty,
so the fee must be routed: the payer's hub (for instance a Lightning
service provider coordinating fee payment to the venue) offers the same lock onward,
to member $i$ or to the next hop, locked to the same point $T$ (less a
routing fee), and when member $i$ seals, the revealed $t$ settles each
hop in turn, backwards. This is a \emph{point}-time-locked contract
(PTLC) route, not a hash-locked one: $t$ is a discrete logarithm, the
sum of the attestation's scalars and the sealer's proposer scalar, and
no hash preimage can stand for ``the venue attested exactly this head''.
The payment is held only from submission until the seal, seconds, so it
has the shape of a hold invoice without the liquidity cost of a long
hold. PTLCs are not yet deployed on the Lightning Network; the scheme
relies on them, or on channels that implement the point lock directly,
as ours do (the lock is an ordinary contract output with an adaptor
pre-signature, Section~\ref{sec:graph:fee}). The proof of concept
settles the lock in a single channel, with the sealer as the payer's
counterparty. Sealing is not contingent on the fee arriving: the lock is
the member's reward, so a payer's route (in the demonstration, through
the opponent) cannot be used to censor. This is a rule, not an incentive: a
member whose fee is blocked may rationally decline to seal, and
1-of-$n$ liveness assumes that members honour the rule, their motive
being the venue's franchise as a whole. Fees should therefore not be
routed through the payer's opponent.

\paragraph{Bonds.} Each member locks bitcoin in a timelocked output on
Bitcoin for the duration of its membership. The lock is a sacrifice of
time value, not a slashable deposit in the baseline design: a member
caught contradicting itself (an unsigned seal; a lock completed
without the matching seal; a seal against its own flag) is
ejected, losing its future fee stream while its lock sits out its term.
The lock is not a second penalty beside the lost fees: the coins sit
out their term whether or not the member cheats, so their time value is
the price of the franchise, paid on entry, and what ejection takes is
the franchise itself.
A venue may additionally attach evidence leaves to the bond, spendable
by anyone holding a proof of equivocation; without covenants such a
spend is a fee race, so the bond ends as miner fee, a burn in effect.

The two penalties suit two different kinds of fault. A
\emph{self-contradiction} (two statements under the member's own keys
that cannot both be honest) is provable without reference to time, and
an honest member never produces one as long as it keeps its state: it
records a seal before releasing it, never flags a move it sealed, and
claims a fee only after its seal is out. A member that loses that state
and contradicts itself is in the position of a Lightning node that
loses its channel state and broadcasts a revoked one, and the same
answer applies: keeping state is the operator's duty. Only
self-contradictions are fit for burning. A \emph{timing fault} (a flag
of a move that was in fact published, a seal the record shows as late)
depends on clocks and propagation, and an honest member can commit one
through skew or a delayed message. It is at most grounds for ejection,
and only outside a tolerance for skew and propagation
(Section~\ref{sec:accountable}). Scaling a penalty with how many
members fault together, as Ethereum's proof of stake does~\cite{ethslash},
would separate isolated accidents from coordinated theft; we leave it
as a direction for future work.

\paragraph{The resulting condition.} An equivocation that needs $t$
members is irrational when
\[
  t \cdot \frac{\mathrm{PV}(F)}{n} \;>\; G,
\]
where $\mathrm{PV}(F)$ is the present value of the venue's total fee
stream over the members' remaining terms and $G$ the value the
equivocation can extract. The lock enters as a bound, not a term: a
member locks only if it expects its fees to repay the time value $L$ of
its lock, so $\mathrm{PV}(F)/n \ge L$ for members who joined, and $L$
can be read off the chain by anyone; competition for membership pushes
the two together, as Linus argues for Stakechains~\cite{coins}. Only
the optional evidence leaves, which burn the principal, add a loss of
a different kind. $G$ is an aggregate even for two-party games:
a colluding majority can act against every contract it serves within
one dispute window, and pays its franchise once. It needs a colluding
player in each contract, and a hub, which is the counterparty of many
contracts, is exactly such a player; so $G$ is the value in flight
across the venue, not one pot. For a fixed threshold fraction $t/n$ the
security budget is a fixed share of the venue's fee revenue, whatever
$n$ is: as with Bitcoin's miners, the number of validators buys
decentralisation and liveness, the revenue buys safety.
Section~\ref{sec:security} takes this up, and
Section~\ref{sec:roster} shows how per-contract committees reduce $G$
for a targeted attack to about one pot.


\section{Contract outputs and the dispute graph}
\label{sec:graph}

This section gives the transaction-level view of a game contract, as
implemented in the proof of concept~\cite{lngapcode}: what
sits on each commitment transaction, which transactions are pre-signed
when a channel state is agreed, which are built at dispute time by one
party alone, and how the venue's attestations enter the scripts as
public-key constants. We use tic-tac-toe as the worked instance
(a $48$-byte entry head, at most $M=9$ moves) and quote chess where the
numbers differ. Throughout, $\Pa$ and $\Pb$ are the two players ($\Pa$
white, $\Pb$ black); each holds a payment key $P_\Pa$, $P_\Pb$, and every 2-of-2 leaf below is
\[
  \push{P_\Pa}\ \op{CHECKSIG}\ \push{P_\Pb}\ \op{CHECKSIGADD}\ \push{2}\ \op{NUMEQUAL}
\]
(or \op{NUMEQUALVERIFY} when the leaf continues), with the witness
carrying $\sigma_\Pb$ then $\sigma_\Pa$. Timelocks: $\tsd$ is the
channel's \texttt{to\_self\_delay}, $\dl$ the challenge window and
$\dlp$ the additional delay on prover-favourable paths; move $d$ is due
at $T_d = t_0 + d\ell$ and a depth-$d$ absence claim is admissible once
median-time-past has passed $T_d + m$ (an absolute, time-based
\op{CLTV}). We write $s$ for the venue's key index of move $d$ of this
contract, $s = (c, d)$ (\S\ref{sec:venue}).

\subsection{Keys and venue data fixed at open}
\label{sec:graph:keys}

The two parties agree the contract at an off-chain draft. Beyond the
channel keys, the draft fixes, per depth $d \in 1..M$ (move $d$ is made
by the \emph{mover} $m(d)$, alternating from $\Pa$ at $d=1$; the other
party is the \emph{claimant} $c(d)$):
\begin{itemize}
\item $K^{\mathrm{ref}}_d$, the mover's \emph{refute key}: a Winternitz
  one-time key (w = 16, HASH160) over the pair of venue heads
  $h_{d-1}\,\|\,h_d$ ($96$ bytes, $192$ message digits plus $3$ checksum
  digits; $48$ bytes at $d=1$, where the prior state is the constant
  empty board);
\item $K^{\mathrm{st}}_d$, the mover's \emph{state key}: a Winternitz
  key over the signed region of the entry (the $3$ state bytes for
  tic-tac-toe; $42$ bytes for chess). The venue entry the mover
  publishes carries this signature inline, so anyone can check
  authorship of a published move without the mover's cooperation;
\item $K^{\mathrm{code}}_d$ (mover) and $K^{\mathrm{ccode}}_d$
  (claimant): Lamport keys over the $2$-bit outcome code $X \in \{\text{A wins},
  \text{B wins}, \text{Draw}\}$.
\end{itemize}
From the venue's registry for this contract the draft pins, per move
$d$ (key index $s = (c, d)$):
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{content table} $A_{s,j,v}$, $j \in 0..191$, $v \in
  0..15$: one anticipation point per header chunk per nibble value, all
  under the venue's shared content key (\S\ref{sec:venue});
\item the $n$ members' \emph{proposer points} $P_{i,s}$ and \emph{flag
  points} $F_{i,s}$, $i \in 1..n$, and the flag threshold
  $t = \lceil (n+1)/2 \rceil$.
\end{itemize}
Only the $96$ head chunks of each table ($j \in 80..175$, the entry head
inside the $96$-byte header) are ever embedded in a script; a refutation
reads the head out, not the whole header.

\subsection{The commitment transaction}
\label{sec:graph:commit}

Each channel state $k$ has two commitment transactions, one per
broadcaster, spending the funding output. Beyond \texttt{to\_local} and
\texttt{to\_remote}, the commitment carries one output per open
\emph{contract}: the game output $C$ of value $V$ (both stakes), and,
while a move's attestation fee is in flight, a \emph{fee lock} output
$L$ (\S\ref{sec:graph:fee}). Every contract output is a taproot output
with an unspendable internal key and a tree of named leaves; on the
broadcaster's version every revocable leaf sits beside the standard
revocation leaf
\begin{lstlisting}[style=script]
revoke:  OP_HASH160 (*\push{h_{\mathrm{rev}}}*) OP_EQUALVERIFY (*\push{P_{\mathrm{cp}}}*) OP_CHECKSIG
\end{lstlisting}
where $h_{\mathrm{rev}}$ is the broadcaster's per-state revocation
hash and $P_{\mathrm{cp}}$ the counterparty's payment key, so a revoked
state is swept in full by the counterparty exactly as in Lightning.
Leaves that pay the broadcaster carry an extra $\push{\tsd}\ \op{CSV}\
\op{DROP}$ so that the revocation window is respected; we omit that
prefix below except where it changes the argument.

The game output's tree is
\begin{align*}
  C:\quad & \texttt{revoke},\ \texttt{settle},\
  \texttt{absent}_d\ (d \in 1..M),\ \texttt{exhibit}_d\ (d \in 5..M),\
  \texttt{equiv}_d\ (d \in 1..M).
\end{align*}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=script]
settle:     (*\push{\mathrm{deadline}}*) OP_CLTV OP_DROP  (*\push{\tsd}*) OP_CSV OP_DROP
            2-of-2
absent_d:   (*\push{T_d+m}*) OP_CLTV OP_DROP  2-of-2-VERIFY  OP_1
exhibit_d:  (*\push{T_d+m}*) OP_CLTV OP_DROP  2-of-2-VERIFY  (*proposer fragment*)
            (*two-head readout tied to $K^{\mathrm{ref}}_d$, gated on "terminal"*)
equiv_d:    2-of-2-VERIFY  WOTS-VERIFY((*$K^{\mathrm{st}}_d$*))  (*park digits*)
            WOTS-VERIFY((*$K^{\mathrm{st}}_d$*))  (*restore*)
            (*some digit differs*) OP_VERIFY  OP_1
\end{lstlisting}
\texttt{settle} pays $R(\text{empty board})$ after the contract's
deadline and is the fallback when nobody claims. \texttt{absent}$_d$ is
the claimant's \emph{thin absence claim}: ``the mover published no valid
move $d$ by its due time''. It asserts nothing about venue content, which is
what keeps it two hundred bytes; the venue enters only if the mover
answers. \texttt{exhibit}$_d$ (tic-tac-toe only; chess needs none,
because a mated side simply has no move to refute with) lets the mover
of a terminal move park the attested pair directly and split by its
result. \texttt{equiv}$_d$ pays the non-mover the whole pot on
exhibiting two distinct valid signatures under $K^{\mathrm{st}}_d$: a
Winternitz signature is forced by its message, so two signatures of
different states are a proof that the mover double-signed at that
depth, and no venue data is needed.

\subsection{The claim output and the refutation}
\label{sec:graph:claim}

\texttt{absent}$_d$ is a pre-signed transaction whose single output
$A_d$ carries the claim tree
\begin{align*}
  A_d:\quad & \texttt{refute},\ \texttt{counter}\ (d \ge 2),\
  \texttt{split}_X\ (X \in \{\text{A},\text{B},\text{D}\}: A wins, B wins, draw).
\end{align*}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=script]
split_X:   (*\push{\dl}*) OP_CSV OP_DROP  2-of-2-VERIFY
           LAMPORT-EXPECT((*$K^{\mathrm{ccode}}_d$*), X)  OP_1
counter:   2-of-2-VERIFY  OP_1
refute:    (*\push{P_{m(d)}}*) OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY
           (*\push{P_{1,d}}*) ... (*\push{P_{n,d}}*) (*\push{n}*) OP_PICK (*\push{n{-}1}*) OP_SWAP OP_SUB OP_PICK
                (*\push{n{+}2}*) OP_ROLL OP_SWAP OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY  (*drop $n{+}1$*)
           WOTS-VERIFY((*$K^{\mathrm{ref}}_d$*))               (*192 digits on the stack*)
           AUTHORSHIP((*$K^{\mathrm{st}}_d$*) over the new head's digits)
           OP_TOALTSTACK (*$\times 192$*)
           READOUT((*$A_{d-1,j,\cdot}$*), (*$j \in 80..175$*))          (*prior head*)
           READOUT((*$A_{d,j,\cdot}$*),   (*$j \in 80..175$*))          (*new head*)
           OP_1
\end{lstlisting}
The claimant's \texttt{split}$_X$ is the timeout: after $\dl$ blocks
with no refutation, the claimant reveals its code key to the outcome it
asserts and both pre-signed signatures pay the corresponding
distribution. The mover's \texttt{refute} is the only leaf that touches
the venue, and it does three things in one script:
\begin{enumerate}
\item it names the proposer: the witness supplies an index $i$ and a
  signature under $P_{i,d}$, made with the scalar $p_{i,d}$ that member
  $i$ revealed in the block it sealed (\S\ref{sec:graph:math});
\item it \emph{parks} the two heads: the WOTS verification of
  $K^{\mathrm{ref}}_d$ leaves the $192$ message digits (the nibbles of
  $h_{d-1}\|h_d$) on the stack, and the tied authorship check proves the
  mover's state key signed the new head's state region, so a
  garbage-signed entry cannot be refuted with;
\item it \emph{reads out} the venue: for each head chunk $j$ the script
  pushes the sixteen points $A_{s,j,0..15}$, selects the one indexed by
  the parked digit, and requires a signature under it. Since the digit
  \emph{is} the parked value, the readout ties ``what the venue attested''
  to ``what the mover re-committed'' by construction, one witness
  element per chunk.
\end{enumerate}
The per-chunk readout fragment, with the parked digit coming off the
altstack, is
\begin{lstlisting}[style=script]
OP_FROMALTSTACK  (*\push{A_{s,j,0}}*) ... (*\push{A_{s,j,15}}*)
OP_16 OP_PICK  OP_15 OP_SWAP OP_SUB  OP_PICK      (*select $A_{s,j,v_j}$*)
OP_18 OP_ROLL  OP_SWAP  OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY   (*possession of its secret*)
OP_2DROP (*$\times 8$*)  OP_DROP
\end{lstlisting}
$16 \times 33$ bytes of constants and $20$ opcodes per chunk; $96$
chunks per head. A wrong digit selects a point whose secret nobody
holds, and the leaf aborts.

\texttt{counter} (from $d \ge 2$) is the mover's own thin claim one
depth back, ``the claimant did not move at $d-1$'': the negation of the
claim's dueness. Its output is the depth-$(d-1)$ claim tree verbatim
(without a counter of its own), so a false counter is refuted by the
claimant's attested, signed head at $d-1$ and judged by the ordinary
family, while a true counter means the claim was never due. One level
closes it.

\subsection{The refuted output}
\label{sec:graph:refuted}

\texttt{refute} is pre-signed too (the mover's payment key gates it),
so its output $P_d$ is pinned to the \emph{refuted tree}
\begin{align*}
  P_d:\quad & \texttt{disprove}_\pi\ (\pi \in \Pi_d),\ \texttt{not\_timely},\
  \texttt{split}_X.
\end{align*}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=script]
disprove_(*$\pi$*):  (*\push{\dl}*) OP_CSV OP_DROP  (*\push{P_{c(d)}}*) OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY
             WOTS-VERIFY((*$K^{\mathrm{ref}}_d$*))  PREDICATE((*$\pi$*) over the 192 digits)  OP_VERIFY
             OP_2DROP (*$\times 96$*)  OP_1
not_timely:  (*\push{\dl}*) OP_CSV OP_DROP  (*\push{P_{c(d)}}*) OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY
             (*\push{F_{1,d}}*) OP_CHECKSIG  (*\push{F_{2,d}}*) OP_CHECKSIGADD  ...
             (*\push{F_{n,d}}*) OP_CHECKSIGADD  (*\push{t}*) OP_GREATERTHANOREQUAL
split_X:     (*\push{\dl+\dlp}*) OP_CSV OP_DROP  2-of-2-VERIFY
             LAMPORT-EXPECT((*$K^{\mathrm{code}}_d$*), X)  WOTS-VERIFY((*$K^{\mathrm{ref}}_d$*))
             RESOLUTION(new head) (*$= X$*)  OP_VERIFY  OP_2DROP (*$\times 96$*)  OP_1
\end{lstlisting}
The \emph{disprove family} $\Pi_d$ is the game's rule set expressed as
predicates over the parked tuple (prior state, move, new state): for
tic-tac-toe \texttt{wrong\_slot}, \texttt{cell\_occupied}$_i$,
\texttt{board\_mismatch}$_i$, \texttt{turn\_not\_flipped},
\texttt{status\_mismatch} and so on (about thirty leaves); for chess the
twelve certificate kinds (a piece moving through an occupied square, a
king left in check, \dots) plus a well-formedness leaf, each taking a
small exhibit (a square, an attacker) from the witness.
Appendix~\ref{app:chess} lists every chess predicate, with the layout
of the 192 parked digits it reads, and follows one disproof (a bishop
moving through a pawn) from the parked tuple to the spend. A disprove is
not pre-signed: the claimant builds it at dispute time, copies the
mover's WOTS reveal from the confirmed refutation's witness, signs with
its own key, and takes the pot. \texttt{not\_timely} is the timeliness
disprove (\S\ref{sec:graph:math}): it counts signatures under the
members' flag points and kills a refutation whose move $t$ members
flagged as empty at its deadline. The mover's \texttt{split}$_X$ is
\emph{self-checking}: unlike the claim output's timeout split, it must
also re-park the pair and prove in script that $R(\text{parked new
state}) = X$, because a refutation carries no adjudicated outcome for
a Lightning-style code-mismatch leaf to judge. It fires only after
$\dl + \dlp$, so every disprove has had its window.

\subsection{Pre-signing}
\label{sec:graph:presign}

Every shaded transaction in Figure~\ref{fig:graph} is built and signed
by both parties when a channel state is agreed, on both commitment
versions, and its signatures are exchanged in the same message as the
commitment signature. A script-path sighash commits to the leaf, so
each leaf's transaction needs its own pair of signatures. The counts
are fixed by $M$:
\[
  1 \;+\; M\,(1 + 1 + 3 + 3) \;+\; M \;+\; (M-1)\,(1 + 1 + 3 + 3)
  \;+\; \underbrace{4\,(M-4)}_{\text{tic-tac-toe exhibits}},
\]
that is \texttt{settle}; per depth the claim, its refutation and the
three-plus-three splits; the equivocation exhibit; per depth from $2$
the counter with the previous depth's refutation and splits; and, for
tic-tac-toe, per terminal depth the exhibit with its three splits. With
$M=9$ this is $166$ transactions; for chess with $M=8$ and no exhibit
family, $129$. The disproves and \texttt{not\_timely} are not counted:
they are the challenger's own transactions. Building the graph is
dominated by hashing the refutation scripts (about $100$\,KB each, one
per depth per version); signing $166$ transactions is a few
milliseconds.

\subsection{How the points and signatures connect}
\label{sec:graph:math}

\paragraph{The content attestation.}
The venue's content key is a Schnorr key $X = x G$ shared by its
members. For key index $s = (c, d)$, chunk $j$ and value $v$ the venue derives a
per-statement nonce $r_{s,j,v}$ and publishes the anticipation point
\[
  A_{s,j,v} \;=\; R_{s,j,v} + e_{s,j,v}\,X,
  \qquad R_{s,j,v} = r_{s,j,v}\,G,\quad
  e_{s,j,v} = H(R_{s,j,v},\,X,\,s\,\|\,j\,\|\,v).
\]
Sealing a header $\mathbf{h}$ with nibbles $v_0..v_{191}$ as move $s$
means revealing, for every $j$, the scalar
\[
  a_{s,j,v_j} \;=\; r_{s,j,v_j} + e_{s,j,v_j}\,x,
  \qquad a_{s,j,v_j}\,G = A_{s,j,v_j},
\]
that is, a Schnorr signature on the statement ``chunk $j$ of move $s$
is $v_j$'' whose value is its own verification key. Scripts never
verify these signatures; they verify possession of them. The mover
proving that the venue attested $h_d$ signs the refutation transaction
itself with each revealed $a_{d,j,v_j}$ as a private key, and the
readout's \op{CHECKSIGVERIFY} under $A_{d,j,v_j}$ succeeds exactly when
the venue revealed that scalar. Revealing two values at one $(s,j)$ is
the venue equivocating; it is detectable by anyone holding both blocks
and is the object the flags and the proposer bit attribute.

\paragraph{The proposer bit and the flag.}
Content is a $96$-nibble message and costs a table; a statement with no
content costs one point. Each member $i$ therefore holds two one-bit
keys per move, $P_{i,s} = p_{i,s} G$ (``I sealed move $s$'') and
$F_{i,s} = f_{i,s} G$ (``move $s$ was not published by its due time''),
and reveals the scalar when the statement holds: $p_{i,s}$ in the block
it seals, $f_{i,s}$ as data. A revealed scalar is again a private key,
so the disputing party signs its \emph{own} transaction with it. In
\texttt{refute} the proposer fragment selects $P_{i,d}$ by a witness
index and demands a signature under it, so every refutation names the
member that sealed the move. In \texttt{not\_timely} the claimant
signs under any $t$ of the $F_{i,d}$ and leaves the rest of the
witness slots empty; \op{CHECKSIGADD} counts, an empty signature adds
zero, and the leaf passes iff at least $t$ members flagged. Members
never see the contract or the transaction: they publish scalars, and
whoever disputes uses them. The threshold is a majority because the
flag is one-sided: false emptiness costs $t$ named flaggers, while a
late attestation left standing costs the proposer plus $n-t+1$
silent members, and the two balance at $t = \lceil (n+1)/2 \rceil$.

\paragraph{The parked tuple.}
The refute key is the bridge between the two halves of the script. A
Winternitz signature under $K^{\mathrm{ref}}_d$ on the message
$h_{d-1}\|h_d$ reveals, for message digit $i$ with value $d_i$, the
chain element $\sigma_i = \mathrm{HASH160}^{(d_i)}(\mathrm{sk}_i)$, and
the script checks $\mathrm{HASH160}^{(15-d_i)}(\sigma_i) =
\mathrm{pk}_i$ digit by digit, with the usual checksum digits
preventing a downward forgery. What remains on the stack afterwards is
the $192$ digits themselves. The readout consumes them as the selectors
$v_j$; the disprove predicates and the resolution check read them as
the tuple. Since the key is one-time and the claim, exhibit and counter
contexts are mutually exclusive spends of $C$, the mover can park at
most one tuple per depth, and a disprove built from the confirmed
refutation's witness always judges the tuple the venue attested.

\paragraph{The fee lock.}
A mover pays the member that seals its move, atomically, with a
point-locked payment in the channel (\S\ref{sec:graph:fee}). Let
$C_h = \sum_{j \in \text{head}} A_{s,j,v_j}$ be the sum of the points
the head $h$ selects and $c_h = \sum_j a_{s,j,v_j}$ its discrete log,
which exists exactly when $h$ is attested at $s$. Because the content
key is shared, $c_h$ alone does not say who sealed; the lock's adaptor
point adds the intended proposer's bit:
\[
  T \;=\; C_h + P_{i,s}, \qquad t \;=\; c_h + p_{i,s}, \qquad tG = T.
\]
The payer pre-signs the claim transaction for the payee with a BIP340
adaptor pre-signature: for message $m$ (the claim's sighash), nonce
$k$ with $R' = kG$ and challenge
$e = H(R'+T,\,P_{\text{payer}},\,m)$,
\[
  s' = k + e\,d_{\text{payer}}, \qquad
  \text{verifiable as } s'G = R' + e\,P_{\text{payer}}\ \text{given } T.
\]
Only member $i$ can complete it, $s = s' + t$, which is a valid
signature under nonce point $R'+T$; and once the completed signature is
public the payer extracts $t = s - s'$. The channel machinery sees only
an ordinary point and an ordinary signature.

\subsection{The fee lock output}
\label{sec:graph:fee}

The fee lock $L$ is a second, small contract output on the commitment
while a move's fee is in flight, with the shape of an HTLC:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=script]
revoke:   as above
claim:    [(*\push{\tsd}*) OP_CSV OP_DROP]  2-of-2   (*the payer's half: adaptor under $T$*)
timeout:  (*\push{\mathrm{expiry}}*) OP_CLTV OP_DROP  [(*\push{\tsd}*) OP_CSV OP_DROP]
          (*\push{P_{\text{payer}}}*) OP_CHECKSIG
\end{lstlisting}
\texttt{claim} is pre-signed to the payee's address, the payee's half a
full signature and the payer's the pre-signature above; it is
broadcastable only once completed with $t$. \texttt{timeout} refunds
the payer after \texttt{expiry}. Cooperatively nothing reaches the
chain: the payee, holding $t$, hands it to the payer and proposes the
next channel state with $L$ removed and its value credited, and the
payer's policy accepts iff $tG = T$. A claim with no matching block on
the venue (sealed by nobody, by someone else, or with another head) is
a self-contradiction of member $i$ against the venue's own record, and
is answered by ejection rather than by script. When the sealer is not the
payer's counterparty, each hop of the route carries the same output,
locked to the same $T$, with expiries decreasing towards the sealer as
for HTLCs; the proof of concept builds the single-hop case.

The shape of the costs is the point of the design. A stall, the common

\section{A game from open to close}
\label{sec:lifecycle}

Putting the pieces together for one game, and following the branches of
Figure~\ref{fig:highlevel}:

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[Open.] The players agree the contract in their channel: stakes,
  depth limit $M$, the registry root, and the one-time keys per depth.
  They sign the new commitment and the graph of Section~\ref{sec:graph}.
\item[Honest play.] Each move is an entry published on the venue in its
  time, with its fee paid through the fee lock, and an ordinary channel
  update between the players. At the end, the loser folds: the channel
  is updated to the result and the contract output disappears. Nothing
  reaches Bitcoin.
\item[A stall.] The mover publishes nothing for move $d$. After $T_d+m$
  the claimant broadcasts the absence claim, then after $\delta$ the
  timeout split. Two small transactions, under $400$ vB, at any depth.
\item[A false stall claim.] The mover did publish. He answers with the
  refutation: the venue's attestation of his entry and the previous
  one, his own signature, and the name of the sealing member. The
  claimant has nothing to disprove; after $\delta + \delta'$ the mover's
  checked split pays him.
\item[An illegal move.] The mover published and signed an illegal move,
  and answers a claim with it. The claimant copies the mover's
  re-commitment from the confirmed refutation's witness and spends the
  one disprove leaf the move violates.
\item[A late move.] A member colluding with the mover sealed his move
  after $T_d$. The honest majority flagged move $d$ at $T_d$;
  the claimant spends \texttt{not\_timely} with $t$ flags.
\item[Equivocation by a player.] The mover signed two different moves at
  depth $d$ (for instance, one to the venue and another to the
  counterparty). Two signatures under a one-time key at one depth are
  their own proof; the \texttt{equiv} leaf pays the claimant, with no
  venue data.
\item[A revoked state.] As in Lightning.
\end{description}

The asymmetry of cost is deliberate. The common failure, a stall, is
cheap to prove. The expensive transaction, the refutation, is paid by
the party answering a claim, and a party answers only when it will win.
Winning does not, however, recover what the dispute cost: the
pre-signed fees come out of the pot, so they fall on the winner. A
player about to lose can therefore make a false claim for a few hundred
virtual bytes and force the winner to pay for a refutation of some
36\,kvB, and some hours of delay, at no further cost to itself. The
dispute ends the contract, so this can be done once, not every move; but
it is a lever for extortion, and it puts a floor under the pot below
which an honest refutation does not pay.

\paragraph{Who pays for a dispute.}
Every on-chain dispute exists because someone deviated: stalled,
refused to fold, claimed or countered falsely, or answered with an
invalid or late move. If the contract is correct, the winner of any
on-chain resolution is the party who did not deviate, so ``the loser
pays'' is ``the winner is reimbursed by the loser''. It suffices that
each side adds a \emph{dispute deposit} $D$ to the contract output
beside its stake. Cooperative settlement (a fold, an agreed result, a
refresh) returns each deposit to its owner; an on-chain resolution pays
the winner the whole output, as the splits already do, so the winner
receives the loser's deposit, and that deposit pays the dispute's fees.
An honest claim is never penalised: a real stall has no valid
refutation, so the claimant wins and is reimbursed; the only ways to
drive an honest claimant into an expensive path (an invalid refutation,
a false counter) are deviations that lose to it. A false claim costs the
honest mover nothing net, provided $D$ covers the most expensive path
it can be forced into, about 42\,kvB for chess. The graph is unchanged;
only the contract's value and the cooperative rules change (and
\texttt{settle}, the no-dispute fallback, returns each deposit). The
proof of concept implements this; with each side depositing 10,000\,sat,
a false claim's three hops of fees are paid from the claimant's deposit
and the honest mover receives both stakes and its own deposit back. The price is
capital: pre-signed fees are bumped at dispute time, so $D$ must be set
at a pessimistic feerate, and both players lock it for the game's
duration, recovering it in the cooperative case. The pot floor becomes a
capital cost rather than a loss, and shrinking the refutation (most of
it is the readout's script constants) reduces it directly. A player
about to lose can still delay the winner's payout, at the price of its
whole deposit.

\section{Beyond games: a public clock and a seal}
\label{sec:beyond}

\subsection{Two different services}

The argument so far rested on one impossibility: between two parties,
whether a message was delivered cannot be decided by anyone else. The
receiver can always deny receipt, the sender can always claim to have
sent, and there is no evidence of non-receipt; this is the fair-exchange
impossibility without a trusted third party~\cite{pagnia}. A contract
therefore cannot punish silence unless messages go somewhere the judge
can see. That somewhere must provide two things together: a record of
what was made available (publication), and deadlines against which
the record is read (a clock). We call the combination a \emph{public
clock}. Bitcoin is one, at the cost of a transaction per message; the
venue is one off-chain.

A single Bitcoin block provides three things at once: a clock, a
publication, and uniqueness (one chain, one spend per output). Because
they arrive together they are easily conflated. Off-chain they separate:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A public clock} is what a two-party game needs. The
  venue's per-move record must be \emph{decided} (the flag majority), but
  no reader other than the two players ever consults it, so nothing is
  gained by one player being shown a different record than the other: the
  players' own one-time keys already forbid them from signing two
  versions of a move.
\item \textbf{A seal} is what a \emph{shared fact} needs: a fact that
  many contracts read, such as the owner of a name or the set of bids in
  an auction. If the venue could show one record to one contract and a
  different record to another, each would settle on a different world.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Shared facts, and what defends them}
\label{sec:shared}

A true single-use seal (a Bitcoin output) reaches a contract only
through a spend in the contract's own inputs; that is how Lightning's
funding output works. A fact carried as \emph{data}, whether a venue
attestation, a Merkle root or a Bitcoin \texttt{OP\_RETURN}, can only
be exhibited to a script, and a script cannot tell which of two
conflicting exhibits is canonical without verifying the chain it came
from, which is impractical. A seal on Bitcoin (anchoring the venue's
record in a single-use output chain) therefore gives uniqueness to
\emph{clients} running nodes, but not to contracts.

For contracts, uniqueness of a shared fact is economic: two attestations
for one fact are an unambiguous, attributable equivocation by the venue,
whichever of them was anchored, and the condition of
Section~\ref{sec:venue} must hold with $G$ the value extractable from
\emph{all} contracts relying on the fact. For a single two-party game $G$ is one
pot, though a majority acting against many games at once faces the
same aggregate (Section~\ref{sec:venue}). For a shared fact it is an aggregate, measured against the
franchise of the members who equivocate. We take this as the limitation
of the system for shared facts: they are secure up to a value set by the
venue's fee revenue, bounded, and best suited to facts whose total
reliance is bounded and known when they are published. An auction is
such a fact; a long-lived registry is not.

\section{Storage contracts: Filecoin without an altcoin}
\label{sec:storage}

We return to the example of the introduction.

\paragraph{Set-up.} The customer erasure-codes and encrypts its data,
splits it into chunks of a few hundred bytes, and computes a Merkle root
$R$ over them. A contract in the customer's channel with the provider
fixes $R$, the number of chunks, an epoch length, a fee per epoch and
the provider's bond $B$. The data is handed over off-chain.

\paragraph{Each epoch is two moves.} The customer publishes a challenge,
an index $i$, on the venue. The provider must publish chunk $c_i$ with
its Merkle path to $R$ by the next due time. The fee for the epoch is paid in
the channel as usual; the bond stays locked.

\paragraph{Disputes are the game's disputes.} A missing response is an
absence claim, refuted only by a venue attestation of a timely response.
A wrong response is a disprove leaf that checks a Merkle path (SHA-256,
native in Script; the chunk must fit a 520-byte stack element). A false
claim by the customer is answered by the attested response. The
contract graph is the chess graph with a different disprove family: the
``move'' is a challenge or a response, and the ``rule'' is a path check.

\paragraph{What it proves.} A provider that has discarded a fraction
$f$ of the chunks survives each uniformly chosen challenge with
probability $1-f$. Because the data was erasure-coded, the customer
needs only a sufficient fraction, so a provider passing most challenges
holds a recoverable copy: this is a proof of retrievability in the sense
of Juels--Kaliski and Shacham--Waters~\cite{jk,sw}. It proves
retrievability, not that the provider itself stores the data; for the
customer that is usually what matters. Responses are public on the
venue, hence the encryption. The contract's depth limit bounds its
term; longer terms are renewed by cooperative refresh, and the default
at the horizon must favour the customer.

\paragraph{What is new.} Paying for proofs of retrievability over
Bitcoin has been proposed before: contingent service payments on
Bitcoin for exactly this~\cite{zkcsp}, recurring variants~\cite{rcpor},
periodic challenge-and-pay over a payment channel~\cite{scrypt}, and
per-download payments with a bond and fraud proof over
Lightning~\cite{bitstream}. All are \emph{pay on success}: if the
provider does not answer, the customer does not pay, and the only
penalty for losing the data is lost future income. No two-party
construction can do better, because silence cannot be adjudicated
between two parties. With a public clock ``no timely response in epoch
$k$'' is a fact, so the provider's \emph{bond} is forfeited for absence,
not merely its payment withheld, and the whole schedule runs off-chain.

\paragraph{Sia and Filecoin.} The dedicated storage networks do punish
absence, and they can because each has its own blockchain, which gives
them the public clock. A Sia host posts collateral in a file contract
and must submit a storage proof for a randomly chosen segment within a
proof window at the contract's end; if it misses the window its
collateral and earned revenue are burned, and the renter recovers only
its unspent payment~\cite{sia}. A Filecoin storage provider posts pledge
and deal collateral and must answer a Proof-of-Spacetime challenge for
every sector every day; a missed proof puts the sector in fault, with a
daily fee, and persistent faults terminate it and slash the collateral,
which is largely burned~\cite{filecoin}. Both burn rather than
compensate. In Filecoin the reason is a third party: stored data earns
storage power and block rewards, so a client and provider who could
profit from penalties together would be taking from the network. A
bilateral contract has no third party to defraud, and paying the
customer from the bond is sound. What it does create, as Sia's design
anticipates, is the customer's interest in the provider failing, by
disrupting its connectivity or by colluding with the venue to suppress
its responses; the second is the venue-majority attack of
Sections~\ref{sec:security} and~\ref{sec:accountable}, and the first is
answered by the provider's freedom to submit to any member, and to
publish on Bitcoin in distress. And in both, the penalty
deters but does not recover data: durability comes from erasure-coding
across many independent providers (Sia's renter software spreads each
file across dozens of hosts and repairs as they fail), with frequent
challenges to detect a loss while repair is still possible. The same
holds here: the contract's role is deterrence and early detection, one
contract per provider. What we add is the absence-adjudicating clock
without a blockchain or a token of its own: the venue provides it, and
settlement stays in bitcoin, in a channel.

\section{Hidden information: blackjack}
\label{sec:blackjack}

A public-information game such as chess demonstrates the mechanism, but
it is a thin market for money. The games people play for money hide
information, and whether LN-GAP can host them is the more telling test.
Blackjack is the simplest casino game that needs everything the venue
provides, and it needs little else. It is designed here, not built;
the one new Script component has been verified on regtest (the
\texttt{size\_commit} test of the proof of concept~\cite{lngapcode}).

\paragraph{The game.} A player plays against a dealer (the house).
Cards count at face value from 2 to 10, ten for a jack, queen or king,
and an ace counts one or eleven, whichever helps. The player receives
two cards face up, the dealer one face up and one face down (the
\emph{hole} card). The player then repeatedly chooses to \emph{hit}
(take a card) or \emph{stand}; going over 21 loses at once. When the
player stands, the dealer reveals the hole card and draws by a fixed
rule: take cards until the total is at least 17. Whoever is closer to 21
without going over wins; equal totals are a tie. We omit the refinements
of casino play (3:2 for a natural 21, doubling, splitting, insurance).

\paragraph{Why it fits.} The dealer makes no decisions: its play is
fixed by the rule. So the hidden information is not anyone's secret
strategy, only the cards not yet dealt, and the protocol must guarantee
three things: each card is random and neither side can bias it; nobody
learns a card before it is dealt, the house included; and the rules,
the dealer's among them, are followed. The first two are commit-reveal
randomness; the third is a disprove family of small arithmetic. What
commit-reveal cannot do on its own is stop a party who sees that a card
will go against it from refusing to reveal its share. That is exactly
what the venue does: a reveal missing at its due time is a stall, and a
stall loses the pot and, with dispute deposits, pays for the dispute.

\paragraph{Randomness.} At open, for each card position $k = 1..K$
($K \approx 16$ suffices for a hand) the player commits to a share
$a_k$ and the house to a share $b_k$, each in $0..12$; card $k$ is
$(a_k + b_k) \bmod 13$, a rank from ace to king. One honest party's share
is uniform and fixed before the other's is known, so the card is
uniform, and neither party knows it until both shares are revealed.
Checking a share against its commitment in Script is the one new
component, since the obvious commitment, a hash of the value and a
salt, cannot be opened without concatenation. Instead the value is
encoded in the \emph{length} of a random string, as in the Bitcoin
lottery of Andrychowicz et al.~\cite{smcbitcoin}: commit
$C = \mathrm{SHA256}(s)$ with $|s| = 32 + v$ bytes; to open, reveal $s$,
and the script checks the hash and reads $v$ with \op{SIZE}. The string
is random and the hash hides its length; a second opening of a
different length would be a SHA256 collision (the 80-bit collision
bound of HASH160 is too low for a committer with time to grind). The
dealing check, which opens both shares, bounds each to $0..12$ and
computes the card, is a 137-byte leaf; on regtest a correct spend is
183\,vB, and a wrong preimage, a string one byte longer, an
out-of-range share and a wrong claimed card are each rejected.

\paragraph{Play.} The game is a sequence of venue entries, each signed
by the side whose turn it is, exactly as chess moves are. To deal card
$k$, both parties reveal their shares, in a fixed order; the player's
cards and the dealer's up-card are dealt first, the hole card's shares
only when the player stands. The player's entries are ``hit'' and
``stand''. In the dealer phase the house reveals the hole card and then
keeps drawing, both shares per card, until its total reaches 17; its
entries are forced. Every entry carries the claimed new state (the
cards so far and the totals), and the last states the outcome, which a
fold then settles cooperatively in the channel.

\paragraph{Disputes.} The disprove family covers: a share that does not
open its commitment; a card computed wrongly from its shares; a total
updated wrongly, including the soft ace; an action where none is
allowed (hitting after a bust or a stand); the dealer standing below 17
or hitting at 17 or above; and a wrong outcome. Each is a comparison or
a few additions, of the size of tic-tac-toe's leaves. Timeliness is the
venue's, as in any game.

\paragraph{Simplifications.} Cards are drawn from an infinite deck (each
rank independently, ten-value cards four times as likely), which is close
to the six- or eight-deck shoes casinos use and avoids shuffling in
Script altogether; dealing without replacement would need a mental-poker
shuffle, which is much heavier. Payouts are one to one. A session of many
hands is one contract with a running balance, within the horizon of
Section~\ref{sec:security}.

\paragraph{Why it matters.} ``Provably fair'' online casinos let a
player verify, after the fact, that the outcomes were honestly random,
while the casino holds the player's money. Here the house never holds
the player's funds, the cards are unbiasable, and the rules, the
dealer's included, are enforced by the contract: provably trustless
rather than provably fair. The house keeps its statistical edge, which
comes from the dealer acting last, and that is legitimate. Babilonia
\cite{babilonia} makes the same move for a one-shot bet, with a
different emphasis: a single wager, resolved by an adaptor signature
whose completion reveals the outcome, with zero-knowledge proofs that
the dealer's hidden choice is well formed, and indistinguishable on
chain from an ordinary payment. A one-shot bet needs no per-step clock,
since Bitcoin's own timeout resolves its only step; blackjack has a
decision at every card, and so needs the venue. The two combine: where
one side genuinely holds a secret, as in poker, Babilonia-style reveals
by adaptor completion could replace the length commitments.

\section{A sealed-bid auction}
\label{sec:auction}

An auction is the simplest useful contract that is not two-party. A
seller auctions a lot; each bidder, and the seller, has a channel with a
hub $\Hub$; every party's contract is a two-party contract with $\Hub$,
and all of them settle on one shared fact: the set of bids published on
time.

\paragraph{Phases.} Bidders publish commitments
$H(\mathit{bid}\,\|\,\mathit{salt}\,\|\,\mathit{key})$ by a commit time
$T_c$ and reveal them by a reveal time $T_r$. A bidder locks
$L_i \ge \mathit{bid}_i$ in its contract, over-locking by a random amount
so that $\Hub$ learns nothing before the reveals. An unrevealed
commitment forfeits a deposit $\pi$. On the keyed venue each bidder's
commitment and reveal are entries under their own keys, one table per
(auction, bidder, phase), so each has exactly one legitimate content and
per-bidder timeliness comes from the flags as for a game; this needs the
bidders known at registration. What the venue does not provide is the
\emph{set}: that these are all the bids, and that every reading contract
sees the same ones.

\paragraph{The outcome is a pointer claim.} Settlement is claimed as
``winner $w$ at price $p$, where $p$ is bid $k$'', not computed. The
claim is refuted by exhibiting a timely revealed bid above $w$'s, a
timely revealed bid other than $w$'s above $p$, or by showing that bid
$k$ is not a timely reveal. Each refutation is one inclusion path and
one comparison; the number of bidders never enters a leaf. Completeness
comes from the same leaves: an omitted bid is exhibited with its
timeliness, and the seller's contract can exhibit \emph{any} omitted
bid, so $\Hub$ cannot suppress the high bid in favour of a shill.

\paragraph{The legs.} As with a routed payment, the hub's contracts net
to zero: in the seller's contract the lot goes to $\Hub$ against $p$, and
in the winner's the lot goes to the winner against $p$; losers are
refunded. The legs are atomic because they settle on the same fact.

\paragraph{Delivering the lot.} If the lot is a Bitcoin-native asset
(RGB or Taproot Assets), it is a Bitcoin output: the seller commits it
into the contract output at open and delivery is a branch of the
pre-signed graph, so the seller cannot withhold it later. Each graph
transaction the asset passes through carries its asset transition,
built in advance using outputs of the same transaction as seals; the
asset's history is published on the venue at set-up so the winner can
validate it. If the lot lives on another chain, the auction sells the
right to be the counterparty of an atomic swap at price $p$, and the
seller's option to walk away can only be priced, by a deposit forfeited
on timeout.

\paragraph{Its security.} The bid set is a shared fact. An equivocating
venue could show the seller's contract a high price and the winner's a
low one, at the hub's expense. That is the limitation of
Section~\ref{sec:beyond}, and here it is benign: the value at stake is
bounded by the auction and known when its phases are registered, so the
fees for its attestations can be sized to it.

\paragraph{What is still to build.} Per-bidder registration, the pointer-claim
graph, and settlement at a variable price (either a grid of pre-signed
splits, one per price bucket, or payouts decomposed into binary-weighted
outputs).

\section{Arbitrary programs: disputing a zero-knowledge verifier}
\label{sec:zk}

Every game so far has a hand-written disprove family: the rules are
small enough that each way of breaking them fits a leaf. An arbitrary
program has no such family, and its execution is far too long for any
script. BitVMX~\cite{bitvmx} gives the standard answer for Bitcoin. The
program runs on a RISC-V CPU whose execution trace is a hash chain,
$h_i = \mathrm{BLAKE3}(h_{i-1} \,\|\, w_i)$ truncated to 20 bytes, where
$w_i$ is step $i$'s 13-byte write record (address, value, next program
counter, micro-step). A prover claims that the program halts with
success at step $N$ with final hash $h_N$. A challenger who disagrees
runs an $n$-ary search with the prover: in each round the prover posts
the hashes at $n-1$ evenly spaced steps of the disputed interval, and the
challenger picks the sub-interval where they first diverge from its own.
After about $\log_n N$ rounds the dispute is one step, whose predecessor
hash both parties accept, and a single-step check decides it. In
BitVMX every move of the search is a Bitcoin transaction. Here the
moves are the game's moves on the venue, like any other: a stalled
round is an absence claim, and only the last step reaches the chain.

\paragraph{The last step is proved, not disproved.} BitVMX's step
scripts are written for the prover: a chain of assertions (the opcode
is of this instruction class, the reads come from the registers the
opcode names, the memory witness matches) followed by an equality
between the committed write and the computed one. Inverting only the
final equality gives a disprove leaf that is sound but not complete: an
assertion that fails aborts the spend, and Script has no conditional
way to succeed after one, so a read record inconsistent with its opcode
fires no leaf. At the final step we therefore use BitVMX's orientation.
The prover's refutation parks the disputed step's record across the
pair of heads, and the refuted output offers the prover a
\texttt{prove} leaf per instruction class after $\delta$ (BitVMX's
script unchanged, then the step's hash checked with BitVMX's BLAKE3),
and the claimant a timeout after $\delta+\delta'$. The rest of BitVMX's
verifier challenges become the claimant's disproves on the same output:
the program counter read is the previous step's written one, the opcode
is the program's instruction at that address, a read value is the last
value written there (after a second search for the write), the halt,
and reads of the input and of initialised memory. These are not yet
built; until they are, a prover that lies about a read value, with an
execution consistent with the lie, passes.

\paragraph{What the proof of concept does.} The program is BitVMX's
Groth16 verifier, a RISC-V build of a pairing-based verifier, checking
a RISC0~\cite{risc0} proof that a STARK receipt of a guest program is
valid. On a genuine proof it halts with success after 478{,}727{,}216
steps; an 8-ary search takes ten rounds to reach the final
\texttt{ecall}, and the prover's proof of it confirms on regtest at
$22{,}736$ vB (a \texttt{prove} leaf is about 86\,KB of script, peak
stack 700). With one bit of the proof flipped the verifier halts with
failure, the prover cannot prove the halting step, and the claimant's
timeout confirms at $137$ vB. The search itself runs between the two
parties in one process, using BitVMX's own search code, not yet as venue
moves: a round carries $n-1$ hashes of 20 bytes (seven in the 8-ary
search above), more than our 48-byte heads hold, so it needs either larger heads or a head that carries a
digest of data travelling in the entry body. (The verifier program's
public constants are set to the RISC0 version of the available proof.)

\subsection{Claims that carry their own proof}
\label{sec:zkclaims}

A zero-knowledge verifier changes what a first move can be: not ``I
am owed $X$'' but ``I am owed $X$, and here is a proof''. This bears on
shared facts (Section~\ref{sec:shared}), whose difficulty was that
their truth could only be attested.

If a claim's truth is a function of data fixed when the contract opens
(the program or verifying key, the keys of any issuer, a genesis
commitment) and of the prover's own witness, no fact is shared between
contracts at run time. The venue attests only when each contract's
moves were published, and the aggregate gain that bounds a shared
fact's security does not arise; the per-contract majority on timing
remains, as for any game.

A bridge is not wholly of that kind. An entitlement to withdraw is a
fact about a history that many parties extend after the contract opens,
and setup can fix only the rules for recognising that history. Two
questions remain. The first is which history is canonical, and a bridge
has two histories to settle. The other system's own order, its
transfers and withdrawals, is typically fixed by a sequencer or a
committee signing its states: their keys are one more setup fact, and
the trust moves to them, with the same answers as for the venue (a
bond, and the data published where others can see it). The deposits,
though, are Bitcoin transactions, and whether they happened is not the
sequencer's to decide: one crediting a deposit that exists only on a
fork would mint entitlements from nothing. That history is decided by
proof of work, which is self-certifying: accumulated work is evidence,
not testimony, like publication. The proven statement includes the
deposit's inclusion under a chain of headers, and a challenger may
answer with a heavier chain, as a move of the game, so the question is
settled by the game and the venue's clock rather than by the venue
vouching for a fact. This is how BitVM2 bridges treat it~\cite{bitvm2},
and why ESSPI's authors note that an inclusion proof must admit
counter-proofs within a bounded time~\cite{esspi}. The second question
is uniqueness: an entitlement proved in two contracts would be paid
twice, which is the shared-fact failure again. It is answered inside
the proven statement, by binding the entitlement to the contract that
pays it (the contract's identifier a public input of the proof). A
stale history is not a separate danger if a withdrawal is a permanent
burn on the other system, since every canonical history containing the
burn will do.

Zero-knowledge claims therefore do not abolish shared facts; they
convert them into setup facts, self-certifying evidence and bindings
inside the proven statement, none of which the venue has to attest. A
shared fact is a problem for this design only when its truth can merely
be attested; when it can be proved, it is one more move of the game.
The practical costs remain: the claim carries the proof, 256 bytes and
its public inputs, which again needs more than a 48-byte head; the
other system's data must be available to the claimant and to any
challenger; and a counter-proof must be published before its deadline,
the same assumption on the venue's timing and on Bitcoin's liveness as
everywhere else.

\subsection{Channels instead of bridges}
\label{sec:bridges}

A bridge moves bitcoin into a system that can express richer programs;
this design moves the programs to the bitcoin. Most of the globality of
a bridge follows from the first choice. Its deposits are pooled in
jointly controlled outputs, and the pool's contract cannot see Bitcoin,
so every withdrawal must convince it, through a proof, that the deposits
and the other system's state lie on the canonical chain; the setup grows
with the operators and the deposits, and the pool nets the risk of
everyone who used it.

In a channel the funds never leave Bitcoin. A deposit is a funding
output that each party checks with its own node, as in Lightning, so no
script is ever shown that a deposit happened; each user's claim is
backed by the collateral in its own channel, and a unilateral close
puts it on chain with the contract attached. Nothing is pegged, so
nothing is pegged out. What many parties share is reached through hubs:
a statement about the hub's own accounting is a bilateral claim against
the hub, provable in the user's contract (Section~\ref{sec:zkclaims}),
and a fact of another channel, such as a payment made through the hub,
crosses by Lightning's own device, a secret whose revelation in one
channel is evidence in the next. Every fact a contract needs is then
bilateral, fixed at setup, self-certifying, bound inside a proven
statement, or a revealed secret, and the venue attests the content of
none of them.

The price is locality. The venue's majority assumption holds per
contract; liquidity is locked per channel, without the netting that
makes a pool capital-efficient; and a contract can resolve only local
state, so an application that needs a total order across many parties
(price-time priority, first-come registration, an auction among many
bidders) places that order with a hub, whose misbehaviour is then
bounded by each channel's collateral rather than threatening a pool. In
return there is far less pooled risk, and far less computation and
on-chain weight in a dispute: nothing outside the channel is ever
proved to Script, and a dispute stays within a constant number of
transactions.

\label{sec:security}

\paragraph{Where the cryptography ends.} Everything about a game's
\emph{content} is cryptographic: a move is legal or a disprove leaf
fires, a player signs once per depth or loses, a revoked state is swept.
Everything about \emph{time} rests on the venue: that a move was
published is a 1-of-$n$ fact, that it was published \emph{on time} is a
majority fact. This split is the design's central trade. Content is
expensive to adjudicate and made cheap by the disprove family; time is
impossible to adjudicate between two parties and made possible by the
venue.

\paragraph{A timekeeper, not a custodian.} A first reading often takes
the design for a federated sidechain with extra steps: if a majority
decides the truth, why not let it decide everything? The answer is in
what the majority decides, and what it holds.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{What it decides.} A federation's keys sign every
  withdrawal, so its majority can move any funds anywhere. The venue's
  committee holds no key to any funds and decides one bit per move. It
  cannot forge a move (the player signs it), change a rule (Script
  enforces it), or pay anyone.
\item \emph{Whom it needs.} A federation majority can steal alone. A
  venue majority gains only by helping one party of a contract against
  the other, so it needs that party as an accomplice, and a dispute
  that reaches the chain and turns on timing.
\item \emph{How much is exposed.} A federation's majority reaches every
  coin in its custody at once. A committee's reaches the pot of a
  contract it serves, in a live dispute, and committees drawn per
  contract (Section~\ref{sec:roster}) make a broad attack need a
  majority of many committees.
\item \emph{Freezing.} Theft is not the only risk, nor the most
  likely: a custodian can refuse to let funds leave, and a federation of
  named legal entities can be compelled to, and can do it easily, for
  instance by admitting withdrawals only to approved addresses. Here
  there is nothing to freeze. Every user's funds sit in its own channel,
  a unilateral close is always available, and if the venue falls
  silent the contracts resolve by their deadlines.
\item \emph{Who the members are.} A federation's members must be known,
  since users trust them by name and their legal exposure is part of
  the security, which is also what makes them compellable. The venue's
  members need no legal identity at all: each is a bonded output and a
  key, and should be pseudonymous. Compelling one achieves little, since
  it holds nothing; the most it can be made to do is misstate the time
  of a move in a contract it serves, under its own key.
\end{itemize}
The machinery of the preceding sections exists to shrink what the
majority decides. Script judges content, one-time signatures bind each
move to its author, publication needs one member, and the majority is
left with a single bit. Without that machinery the majority would decide
outcomes, and the design would be a federation. In a federated sidechain
the majority is judge, jury and bank; here it is a timekeeper, which can
be bribed to misstate the time of one move in one contract, and only in
league with a party to that contract.

\paragraph{Censorship.} A mover whose entry no member will seal loses by
absence. This is theft-shaped, not merely a delay, and nothing in Script
prevents it. It requires every member to refuse until the move is due, and
so is defended by the independence of the members (operators,
jurisdictions, hosts) and by the fees each forgoes; bonds, which punish
only self-contradiction, do not reach it. Publishing the entry on
Bitcoin makes such a refusal publicly visible
(Section~\ref{sec:accountable}).

\paragraph{Bitcoin's liveness.} Every branch of the dispute graph is a
race against a timelock: a refutation must confirm before the
claimant's timeout, a disprove before the mover's split. The contract
therefore assumes, as Lightning does for its HTLCs and BitVMX for its
rounds, that a transaction can be confirmed within its window. The
graph's pre-signed transactions have one input each and pay a fee fixed
at signing, out of that input; this leaves the replacement-cycling
attack~\cite{cycling} no second input to evict them through, but a fee
spike can hold one back past its window. Fee-bumping (an anchor output
for CPFP) would answer the spike and readmit the attack, with
Lightning's mitigations; the windows $\delta$ and $\delta'$ are what
buys time for either.

\paragraph{The majority.} Flags are the only place the design needs a
threshold, and it is there for \emph{order}, not for stake: two blocks
by two members (``move $d$ was not published'', ``here is move $d$'')
cannot be ordered by any third fact, so canonical timing needs agreement
among the members, or an external clock. A colluding majority can kill
an honest mover's refutation with false flags; the flags are signatures,
so the colluders are named, and the residual falls on a party who knows
whom to pursue. Naming is not proof, however: whether a move was
published in time cannot be shown to a third party.
Section~\ref{sec:accountable} describes how to make it so.

\paragraph{Venue failure.} If the venue stops altogether, nothing can
be published, and an absence claim against whoever is on move succeeds.
In the present design a dead venue therefore decides games against the
player on move. Requiring the claimant to show the flags (the positive
statement ``move $d$ was not published'') \emph{in the claim itself} would turn
this into a liveness failure (no flags, no claim; the contract settles by
its default at the deadline), at the price of letting a colluding
majority protect a staller by withholding flags. The choice is between
the two residuals, and it is open.

\paragraph{Economic security, and its limit.} The bond and the fees
defend what is attributable. For one two-party contract the gain from
venue misbehaviour is at most its pot, but a colluding majority can act
against every contract it serves at once, and a hub is a colluding
player in many of them; so the members' fee franchise must exceed the
value in flight across the venue, not the largest single pot.
For shared facts the gain is the sum over all contracts relying on a
fact, and the same franchise must cover it. We state this as the
limitation of the system rather than engineer around it: the only ways
to remove it are verifying the venue's chain in Script (SPV, impractical)
or turning the fact into Bitcoin outputs that each contract spends,
which works only for facts of a few bits fixed in structure long in
advance. Two further courses accept the limit but shrink it: committees
drawn per contract, so that a targeted attack reaches one pot and a
broad one needs a share of a large roster; and accountability rather
than enforcement, making misbehaviour publicly verifiable
(Section~\ref{sec:accountable}).

\section{Weakening the majority assumption}
\label{sec:accountable}

This section describes extensions that are not yet implemented. They
address the one threat of Section~\ref{sec:security} that the design
leaves to economics alone: a colluding majority of the venue. There are
three levers: \emph{whose} majority it is (a committee drawn per
contract from a bonded roster), \emph{how large} it must be to steal
(two-sided votes and a void zone), and whether its lie \emph{can be
shown} (publication on Bitcoin, or an audit layer).

\subsection{The threshold adversary}
\label{sec:threshold}

A majority of the members can take the pot of any contract, in either
direction. By \emph{false flags}, $t$ members declare an honest mover's
timely move unpublished and \texttt{not\_timely} kills its refutation.
By a \emph{late seal with suppressed flags}, one member seals a stalling
mover's move after its due time while $n - t + 1$ members withhold their
flags, so the late refutation stands. The majority threshold balances
the two, each needing about half of the members. This resembles a
majority attack on a proof-of-work chain, with three differences. The
venue's attackers are identified (every flag and every seal carries a
member's key: a pseudonymous identity, not a legal one), where a
miner's work carries no identity at all. What they stake is a
franchise of future fees, bought with a timelocked bond that cannot be
rented for the attack, where hash power can. And the attack can target a single contract,
silently, where a reorganisation is public.

What makes the attack hard to punish is not anonymity but the nature of
the fact decided. Whether a move reached the members in time is exactly
the kind of fact that no third party can check: the impossibility of
Section~\ref{sec:beyond}, which the venue resolved for the two players,
reappears among the members, and is resolved by vote. A timeliness lie
by a majority is therefore attributable (we know who flagged) but not
falsifiable (nobody can show the flag was false). Punishment by ejection
needs an objective basis, and there is none.

\subsection{Committees drawn from a roster}
\label{sec:roster}

The first question is \emph{whose} majority. Two players cannot both
choose a majority of the members who judge them, and today neither
chooses: the venue is one fixed set, and its majority is the same for
every contract. It need not be. The venue is already per contract in
everything but its membership: tables, proposer and flag points, due
times and the registry are all keyed by the contract
(Section~\ref{sec:venue}), and nothing ties two contracts' members
together. We propose that each contract's members, its
\emph{committee}, be drawn at random from a larger \emph{roster} of
bonded members. There is then no single venue but one per contract,
each sampled from the same roster. A member's work falls rather than
rises: it serves only the contracts it is drawn for, about $n/R$ of them
for committees of $n$ from a roster of $R$. The committee's size is
limited by Script (its flag points are counted in a leaf); the roster's
is not.

\paragraph{The draw.} Both players commit to random values before
either knows the result. The seed hashes both values together with the
hash of a Bitcoin block at a height fixed after the commitments, and a
public procedure maps the seed and the roster to a committee. Without
the block hash, whoever reveals last would see the committee first and
could abort and retry until the draw suited it; with it, each attempt
costs a block, and a player that keeps abandoning contracts before
funding is conspicuous. Each player may first strike a small, fixed
number of members it distrusts; a larger allowance would let a player
strike honest members too. The committee is fixed at open, because the
pre-signed graph embeds its points.

\paragraph{What it buys.} An adversary now needs a share of the
roster, not a majority of one set. If it controls a fraction $f$ of the
roster, the chance that a committee of a few dozen has a colluding
majority is small while $f$ is well below a half, and it falls
exponentially with the committee's size; with the void zone of
Section~\ref{sec:voidzone}, where theft needs two thirds, committees of
a few dozen suffice even when $f$ approaches a third. Collusion now
takes one of two forms, and both cost more than before. A \emph{broad}
attack, taking many contracts at once, needs a share of the whole
roster near a half (or two thirds): what a fixed venue's majority
already posed, spread over a larger set. A \emph{targeted} attack,
bribing a particular committee after the draw, reaches only the
contract that committee serves. The aggregate gain $G$ of
Section~\ref{sec:venue}, which for a fixed venue is the value in flight
across every contract (a hub being a colluding player in many of them),
becomes for a targeted attack about one pot, and a hub that wants to
steal from many of its contracts must corrupt as many different
committees.

\paragraph{What it does not buy.} Sampling defeats collusion arranged
before the draw, not bribery after it: the committee is known at open
and serves for the contract's life. It complements, and does not
replace, the measures below, which make a lie by the chosen committee
provable.

\paragraph{The bond is the ticket.} Every guarantee above is a
statement about the adversary's share of the roster, and that share
means something only if it is costly to acquire. Membership of the
roster is therefore a bond, and the bond does more work than in a fixed
venue:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The roster lives on Bitcoin.} A member is a timelocked
  output with its owner's key, announced by a signature under that key.
  The roster at a given height is the set of unexpired announced bonds,
  which anyone with a node can reconstruct. Entry is permissionless,
  as it is for JoinMarket's makers, whose fidelity
  bonds~\cite{fidelity} are exactly such announced timelocked outputs,
  and the draw is reproducible by anyone from the seed.
\item \emph{Weight, not headcount.} The draw weights members by the
  value locked and by the lock's remaining term, so an adversary's share
  of the roster is its share of locked bitcoin-time, not its number of
  identities. Splitting a bond into ten members buys nothing.
  JoinMarket goes further: it values a bond by the time value it
  sacrifices raised to a power above one, so that splitting loses
  weight and consolidating gains it. That trades Sybil resistance
  against concentration in a few large members, and for a roster whose
  purpose is a random, independent committee the linear weighting may
  be the better choice; we leave it open.
\item \emph{The term covers the contract.} A bond must outlast every
  contract its owner serves, including the dispute windows; a member
  whose lock expires before a contract's last deadline is not eligible
  for that contract.
\item \emph{Sizing per contract.} A targeted bribe must pay a majority
  of the committee at least what each member stands to lose: its
  expected fees for the rest of its term. Those fees are not visible,
  but the time value of the member's remaining lock is, and it bounds
  them from below (a member locks only if its fees repay it). The
  players can check that bound against their pot at open, and a contract with a larger pot can
  require a larger committee or a larger minimum bond. The condition of
  Section~\ref{sec:venue} becomes a per-contract parameter rather than
  one inequality for the whole venue.
\item \emph{Size and age.} A player has two levers it can check on
  chain at open: the size of each member's bond, which bounds the
  member's fee income from below, and its age, since a long-standing
  member may earn more than its lock and so has more to lose; weighting
  by age also makes an attacker pay in time as well as capital. Neither buys more
  than a franchise's worth of defence: a player can reasonably expect
  protection for a pot up to about what a committee majority stands to
  lose, and no more.
\item \emph{Assembling versus using.} As in Section~\ref{sec:venue},
  the baseline bond buys a franchise that ejection takes away, and
  ejection needs an objective fault. A timeliness lie is
  attributable but not falsifiable
  (Section~\ref{sec:threshold}), so the bond makes a colluding
  majority costly to \emph{assemble}; making it costly to \emph{use}
  needs the record of the audit layer below.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Two-sided votes and a void zone}
\label{sec:voidzone}

Two changes to the contract, not yet implemented, raise the size of
coalition that can steal.

\emph{Two-sided votes.} Today a member makes one statement per move,
the flag (``not published in time''), and a member that says nothing
counts in effect as saying the move was published; a colluder
protecting a late move can simply stay silent, indistinguishable from an
offline member. With two-sided votes each member holds two one-bit keys
per move, ``published in time'' and ``not published in time'', and
reveals one of them. A colluder must then sign a false statement rather
than hide behind silence; an offline member counts for neither side; and
a member that reveals both has contradicted itself, provably, even in
Script.

\emph{A void zone.} Today a single threshold decides: at least $t$ flags
and the claimant wins, otherwise the refutation stands. With two
thresholds, at least two thirds voting ``not published'' pays the
claimant, at most one third pays the mover, and anything in between
refunds both stakes. Stealing then needs two thirds of the committee in
either direction; a coalition between one and two thirds can only force
a refund. This is the familiar trade of Byzantine agreement, one third
to block progress and two thirds to break safety. In the contract it
costs a second set of $n$ points in the vote-counting leaf and one more
leaf, the refund; its price in play is that an honest winner can be
griefed into a refund by a sizeable minority.

\subsection{Why not publish on Bitcoin?}

The obvious public record is Bitcoin itself. A mover posts its entry in
an \texttt{OP\_RETURN}, confirmed with median-time-past before the
move's due time, and members are required to watch for such postings.
Publication is self-certifying (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}), so flagging a
move that Bitcoin shows as published becomes a lie anyone with a node
can see; if posting is mandatory, absence becomes checkable too.

Mandatory posting is not a solution. It puts every move on Bitcoin in
the honest case, which is the per-move cost the venue exists to avoid.
The cost is not quite that of an on-chain game, since a posting carries
the entry and not the verification of the move, which for a game with
weighty rules is most of the cost; but it is still a transaction per
move, with confirmation times and fee spikes setting the pace of play.

Posting only in distress is more reasonable, as an option. With a long
move window, say two hours, a mover that sees no seal within minutes of
submitting, where it expects one within seconds, posts its entry then.
This happens rarely, and the price is paid in long timeouts rather than
fees. Its reach is limited in two ways. It answers a false flag, since
the posting shows anyone with a node that the move was published in
time; it does not answer the opposite lie, a majority that covers for a
mover by sealing its late move or withholding its flags, because when
posting is optional a missing posting proves nothing, and absence still
has no public witness. And the evidence is for observers, not Script:
the flags still decide the contract, and the posting makes the lie
provable, to be punished outside it (the roster's bond,
Section~\ref{sec:roster}).

Posting on Bitcoin before a deadline is the right tool for a different
job: one large publication per dispute, not one per move.
ESSPI~\cite{esspi} builds it for the program input of
BitVMX~\cite{bitvmx}: a pre-signed kick-off transaction has an output
that the prover must spend, with a transaction carrying the input,
before a relative timelock expires, or a pre-signed penalty takes the
funds. Script is convinced of availability by the timelock, not by a
proof of inclusion. Its authors observe that an inclusion proof, to be
safe at high value, must admit counter-proofs within a bounded time, so
it too rests on transactions not being censored for long, and the
timelock asks for nothing more. The venue's contracts rest on the same
assumption: every claim, refutation and timeout supposes that a
transaction can be confirmed within its window.

Both modes show that a public
record, not Script, is what makes the venue's honesty checkable. The
question is where that record should live.

\subsection{A third layer}
\label{sec:audit}

The record need not live on Bitcoin, and it need not be verified in
Script. Its only purpose is to let observers judge the venue after the
fact, and observers can verify far more than a script can. The
roster's members are its natural participants, long-lived and bonded,
but the roles differ: the roster is who may be drawn, the audit layer is
the record against which the drawn are judged. This suggests three
layers (Figure~\ref{fig:layers}):

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{27mm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{50mm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{27mm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{30mm}}
\toprule
layer & role & verified by & constraint \\
\midrule
Bitcoin & money; the dispute's clock; rare anchoring of the audit layer & Script, everyone & slow, expensive \\
venue committee & per-contract attestations and flags, legible to Script & Script & small: tens to about a hundred members, one-bit statements \\
audit layer & the public record of what was published, and when & observers only & none from Script: large, dynamic \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every entry is published to the audit layer as it is made, cheaply and
quickly; the audit layer commits its history to Bitcoin periodically
(an \texttt{OP\_RETURN} per hour, say, for all contracts at once), which
fixes it against rewriting at an amortised cost. A committee majority
that flags a move the audit layer shows as published, or lets stand a
seal the audit layer shows as late, by more than a tolerance for clock
skew and propagation, is then caught in either direction,
without per-move Bitcoin cost. The pattern is familiar: a small
committee that a constrained verifier can check, attesting to a larger
system that full observers check, as Ethereum's sync committees do for
light clients~\cite{synccommittee}; and a public log whose timeliness
rests on many independent witnesses and on anchoring, as in
witness-cosigned transparency logs~\cite{ct} and
OpenTimestamps~\cite{ots}. Script never verifies the audit layer, so the
limit Script places on quorum size no longer limits the size of the
trusted set; it limits only each contract's committee.

The audit layer can be an existing blockchain chosen for cheap
postings: the mover posts each entry there, and the chain's own
timestamps and finality become the record against which the committee
is judged. What that chain must offer is availability of the posting,
finality within the claim margin, and freedom from censorship within
the move window, since a mover whose posting is excluded until its due
time forfeits. It does not remove the committee: Script cannot read the
other chain's consensus, so someone must still attest, in keys that
Script checks, what the chain shows. The contract remains denominated
in bitcoin while the timeliness of its moves rests on another system's
trust model, much as a DLC's payout rests on its oracle.

\subsection{What remains}

A committee majority, now two thirds, can still take a pot. With an
audit layer it can do so only provably, at the cost of every colluder's
franchise; security remains economic at the top, like proof of work,
but the attack is no longer deniable. One step further is possible: if
committee members' bonds are held under a key controlled by the roster
(a large-threshold key over its membership), the roster could
slash a provably colluding committee and compensate the victim, judging
from the audit layer's record. That moves the trust up a level, to the
larger set, which is the point.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

ForceMove~\cite{forcemove} and state channels are the paradigm we
modify; Lightning~\cite{lightning} the base we build on. Single-use seals
and proof of publication are due to Todd~\cite{toddseals}, and
OpenTimestamps~\cite{ots} is their simplest deployment; RGB~\cite{rgb}
builds client-side-validated assets on them, and its companion Prime
design places commitments in a dedicated header chain anchored to
Bitcoin. The bonded, Bitcoin-collateralised validator set with one-time
signatures is Linus's Coins and Stakechains~\cite{coins}; what is new
here is that the venue settles no value and carries no asset, and that
its attestations are read in Bitcoin Script. BitVM~\cite{bitvm} settles
arbitrary computation on Bitcoin by bisection, and BitVMX~\cite{bitvmx}
does so for a RISC-V CPU whose execution trace is a hash chain. There
the search itself is on chain: a dispute is a graph of transactions
pre-signed by both parties at setup, emulating covenants, in which each
move of the search (the prover's hashes, the challenger's choice) is a
transaction, the parties' data are carried in
Winternitz signatures that link the rounds, and each round is guarded
by a relative timelock that a party who does not answer loses by. This
is ForceMove played on Bitcoin with the rounds as moves. It needs no
trust beyond Bitcoin's eventual freedom from censorship, and a dispute
costs a number of transactions, and of timelock waits, logarithmic in
the length of the trace. We avoid on-chain bisection by giving each
game a family of single-step disprove leaves; the same search could
instead be played as a venue game, with only its final step on chain,
which Section~\ref{sec:zk} develops for a zero-knowledge verifier.
StackSat-128~\cite{stacksat} is a Script-friendly hash designed for the
proof-of-work alternative. The attestation primitive is that of Discreet
Log Contracts~\cite{dlc}: an oracle publishes anticipation points in
advance and reveals the scalar for the outcome that occurred, and a
contract conditions on it; the venue is a federation of such oracles,
attesting publication and timeliness rather than external events, and
its attestations are read out in Script rather than consumed by adaptor
signatures alone. The accountable venue of
Section~\ref{sec:accountable} follows the pattern of Ethereum's sync
committees~\cite{synccommittee} (a sampled committee attesting for a
constrained verifier) and of witness-cosigned transparency
logs~\cite{ct}; committee sampling by joint randomness is in the spirit
of Algorand's sortition~\cite{algorand}. For storage, see
Section~\ref{sec:storage}; for hidden-information games and Babilonia, Section~\ref{sec:blackjack}.

\section{Conclusion}

Lightning cannot adjudicate silence, and that single gap is what keeps
games, service agreements and auctions off it. A venue that publishes
each move as it is published, attests it with signatures a Bitcoin script can
read, and flags by majority what it did not, closes the gap with
$O(1)$ transactions per dispute and no change to Bitcoin. Content stays
cryptographic; time becomes a 1-of-$n$ and majority fact about a bonded,
fee-paid set of members; shared facts are secured economically, up to a
bound set by the venue's revenue. A proof of concept, with the venue,
the dispute graph for tic-tac-toe and chess, their scenario suites on
regtest, and an interactive chess demonstration in the browser, is
available~\cite{lngapcode}. The remaining weakness, a colluding
majority of the venue, can be made both harder to assemble and
accountable rather than deniable: committees drawn per contract from a
large bonded roster, and an audit layer, anchored to Bitcoin, against
which their statements can be judged.

\section{The chess disprove family, in full}
\label{app:chess}

Section~\ref{sec:graph:refuted} gives the shape of a
\texttt{disprove}$_\pi$ leaf and names the predicates only by example.
This appendix lists every predicate of the chess family, says what each
computes and from which digits, and works through one disproof from the
parked register file to the spend. The code is the
\texttt{lngap-chess} crate (\texttt{certificate.rs} for the
statements, \texttt{leaf.rs} for their Script) and the PoS glue in
\texttt{lngap-pos} (\texttt{chess.rs})~\cite{lngapcode}.

\subsection{What a disprove leaf sees}

A confirmed refutation at depth $d$ has parked, under the one-time key
$K^{\mathrm{ref}}_d$, the pair $\mathrm{head}(d-1)\,\|\,\mathrm{head}(d)$:
$2 \times 48$ bytes, so $192$ hexadecimal digits (nibbles, high nibble
of each byte first). The disprove leaf re-verifies that Winternitz
signature (\texttt{WOTS-VERIFY}), which leaves the $192$ digits on the
stack as the \emph{register file}, and evaluates its predicate over
them. At $d = 1$ there is no prior head on chain; the leaf pushes the
constant initial head in its place, so the predicate always sees two
heads.

A head is three parts:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
bytes & digits (in a head) & content \\
\midrule
0--3 & 0--7 & word0: game id (2 bytes, big-endian), depth, mover \\
4--7 & 8--15 & word1: the move, $(\text{promo} \ll 12 \mid \text{to} \ll 6 \mid \text{from}) \ll 16$ \\
8--47 & 16--95 & the state, 40 bytes (below) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In the register file the prior head occupies digits $0$--$95$ and the
new head $96$--$191$, so the prior state starts at digit $16$ and the
new state at digit $112$. The state bytes are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
state byte & state digits & field \\
\midrule
0--31 & 0--63 & the board: one nibble per square, square $s$ at digit $s$ \\
32 & 64, 65 & side to move ($0$ White, $1$ Black) \\
33 & 66, 67 & castling rights (4 bits; the high nibble must be $0$) \\
34 & 68, 69 & en passant square ($64$ = none) \\
35 & 70, 71 & halfmove clock \\
36, 37 & 72--75 & the move's from and to squares \\
38 & 76, 77 & promotion piece in the high nibble; low nibble $0$ \\
39 & 78, 79 & depth \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Squares are numbered $s = f + 8r$ with file $f$ and rank $r$ from $0$,
so a1 $= 0$, h1 $= 7$, a8 $= 56$. A square's nibble is empty ($0$) or
a piece kind $k$ (1 pawn, 2 knight, 3 bishop, 4 rook, 5 queen, 6 king)
plus $8$ for Black. There is no move number in the state (the depth
takes its place), which is why the chess certificate's thirteenth kind,
\textsc{MoveNumber}, is not part of the PoS family.

\paragraph{The common preamble.} Script has no multiplication, division
or bitwise operations, so every chess leaf opens with the same
arithmetic, built from comparisons, additions and doublings:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item a byte from its two digits: $16h + \ell$ as \texttt{h} doubled
  four times, plus \texttt{l};
\item rank and file of a square $s$: $r = \sum_{k=1}^{7} [s \ge 8k]$
  (seven comparisons), $f = s - 8r$ ($8r$ by three doublings);
\item colour and kind of a nibble $n$: $c = [n \ge 8]$, $k = n - 8c$;
\item a board read at a computed square: \texttt{<base> OP\_SWAP
  OP\_SUB OP\_PICK}, the square having been range-checked to $0..63$
  (\texttt{OP\_WITHIN OP\_VERIFY}) whenever it comes from the witness.
\end{itemize}
From these the preamble names: the side to move $S$ (from the
\emph{prior} state), $\Delta f = f(\text{to}) - f(\text{from})$,
$\Delta r = r(\text{to}) - r(\text{from})$, and the kind $K$ of the
piece on from in the prior board, or $0$ if that square does not hold a
piece of side $S$. from, to and the promotion come from the
\emph{new} state's bytes 36--38 (the mover's own account of its move).

\paragraph{The general form.} Every leaf of the family has the shape
of \S\ref{sec:graph:refuted}:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=script]
(*\push{\dl}*) OP_CSV OP_DROP  (*\push{P_{c(d)}}*) OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY      (*the claimant, after the window*)
WOTS-VERIFY((*$K^{\mathrm{ref}}_d$*))                             (*the 192 parked digits*)

\subsection{The predicates}

Each chess predicate is the \emph{negation} of one statement a legal
move makes true; the leaf fires (leaves $1$) exactly when the parked
tuple falsifies it. ``Prior'' and ``new'' are the two parked states;
$S$ is the side to move in the prior.
\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\ttfamily}p{0.28\linewidth}p{0.49\linewidth}p{0.13\linewidth}@{}}

  slot: (game, $d-1$, the claimant) and (game, $d$, the mover) & -- \\
chess\_malformed & the new head is not a well-formed encoding: from or

The first two leaves are about the heads as records (the right slot,
a decodable encoding); \texttt{chess\_malformed} uses only digit
comparisons and never reads the board, so it stays live for a head
whose fields would make the other leaves' reads fail. The twelve
\texttt{chess\_}$\ast$ kinds are the rules. Together they are designed
to be \emph{sound} (a legal move with its honest successor fires no
leaf) and \emph{complete} (any other well-formed tuple fires at least
one, with some exhibit); in the code the first is a unit test over
legal moves and the second is tested by fuzzing against an independent
move generator. The exhibit is what makes each check bounded: king
safety is a universal (``no enemy piece attacks the king''), and its
falsification is one attacker, which the leaf verifies directly rather
than searching.

The exhibits range over small sets: $j \in \{1,\dots,6\}$,
$\mathit{crossed} \in \{0,1\}$, and squares in $0..63$, range-checked
in the leaf. A claimant who picks a wrong exhibit (a $j$ pointing at
an empty square, an attacker that does not attack) simply fails the
leaf and can try another before the window closes; the challenge space
is about $4{,}400$ (kind, exhibit) pairs and the claimant searches it
off-chain.

\subsection{A worked disproof: a bishop through a pawn}

This is scenario PC6 of the repository, run on regtest.

\paragraph{The moves.} $\Pa$ (White) plays 1.\,e4, sealed at depth 1.
At depth 2 $\Pb$ (Black) submits c8--e6: the bishop on c8 moves along
the diagonal through d7, which holds Black's own pawn. $\Pb$ signs the
entry and publishes, as the new state, exactly what that move would
produce if it were legal: the bishop on e6, c8 empty, side to move
White, castling rights unchanged, en passant none, halfmove clock $1$.
The venue attests it (the venue checks signatures, not chess).

\paragraph{The dispute.} $\Pa$ will not play on from an illegal
position. Once the claim lock $T_2 + m$ has passed it broadcasts the
thin absence claim \texttt{absent}$_2$ ($189$ vB), ``$\Pb$ did not move
at depth 2''. As a statement about the venue the claim is false, and
$\Pb$ answers with \texttt{refute} ($36{,}615$ vB): the venue's attestation
of heads 1 and 2, the proposer fragments, and the Winternitz
signature under $K^{\mathrm{ref}}_2$ that parks
$\mathrm{head}(1)\,\|\,\mathrm{head}(2)$ in the output $P_2$. The
refutation is valid: the heads \emph{were} attested. What it has done
is put $\Pb$'s move on chain in a form Script can read.

\paragraph{Finding the predicate.} $\Pa$ runs the family off-chain on
the parked tuple. Exactly one leaf fires: \texttt{chess\_ray}, with
$j = 1$. (\texttt{chess\_board} does not: the new board is the honest
consequence of the claimed move. Nor do \texttt{chess\_mover}, since
a bishop may move diagonally; \texttt{chess\_destination}, since e6 is
empty; or the field leaves, since $\Pb$ updated them correctly.)

\paragraph{The computation.} In the register file, with the offsets
above:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
digits & value & meaning \\
\midrule
81 (prior byte 32, low) & 1 & $S$ = Black \\
16 + 58 = 74 (prior c8) & 11 ($= 3 + 8$) & a black bishop \\
16 + 51 = 67 (prior d7) & 9 ($= 1 + 8$) & a black pawn \\
184, 185 (new byte 36) & 3, 10 & from $= 16 \cdot 3 + 10 = 58$ (c8) \\
186, 187 (new byte 37) & 2, 12 & to $= 16 \cdot 2 + 12 = 44$ (e6) \\
188 (new byte 38, high) & 0 & no promotion \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The preamble computes $r(58) = 7$, $f(58) = 58 - 56 = 2$ and $r(44) =
5$, $f(44) = 44 - 40 = 4$, so $\Delta f = 2$ and $\Delta r = -2$. The
prior nibble at 58 is $11$: non-zero, colour $1 = S$, so $K = 11 - 8 =
3$, a bishop. The body of \texttt{chess\_ray} is
\begin{lstlisting}[style=script]
guard := (df = 0 or dr = 0 or |df| = |dr|)  and  (df, dr) != (0, 0)
         and  K != 2  and  j >= 1  and  j < max(|df|, |dr|)
if guard:
    step := sign(df) + 8 * sign(dr)                 (*three doublings*)
    sq   := from
    for i in 1..6:  if i <= j:  sq := sq + step     (*six gated additions*)
    result := prior[sq] != 0                        (*computed \texttt{OP\_PICK}*)
else:
    result := 0
\end{lstlisting}
With the exhibit $j = 1$: the guard holds ($|2| = |-2|$, not a
knight, $1 \le 1 < 2$); $\mathit{step} = 1 + 8 \cdot (-1) = -7$; one
gated addition gives $\mathit{sq} = 58 - 7 = 51$, d7; the read returns
the prior nibble $9$, which is non-zero. The body leaves $1$; the
cleanup drops everything else, and the spend succeeds with that $1$ as
the only stack element. Appendix~\ref{app:chess:bytes} shows the same
computation opcode by opcode.

\paragraph{The spend.} After the refutation has $\dl$ confirmations,
$\Pa$ builds \texttt{disprove\_chess\_ray} spending $P_2$: witness
$[\,1,\ \text{the }195\text{ reveal pairs from the refutation's
witness},\ \sigma_{\Pa}\,]$, the leaf script and its control block. It
confirms at $4{,}955$ vB and pays the whole contract output to $\Pa$.
The three transactions total $41{,}759$ vB. $\Pb$'s checked
\texttt{split}$_X$ on the same output was locked for $\dl + \dlp$, so
the output is gone before it could open.

Had $\Pa$ picked \texttt{chess\_board} instead (a natural first guess
for ``the board is wrong''), the leaf would have failed whatever
square it named; the regtest test \texttt{pos\_chess\_graph} checks
that rejection before mining the ray disprove. The illegality is in the
move, not in the bookkeeping, and the family has one leaf for each.
